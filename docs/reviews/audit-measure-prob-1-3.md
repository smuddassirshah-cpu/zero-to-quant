## Level 1

1. app/content/courses/measure-prob/l1-why-measure.json | screens[3].hints[1].text | imprecise | "Use monotonicity, then countable additivity on the lengths." Countable additivity applies to disjoint sets, and the covering intervals are not disjoint: for every $\varepsilon > 0$ the window round 0 has half-width $\varepsilon/4$ and contains every rational $1/q$ with $q > 4/\varepsilon$, whose own windows sit inside it (for $\varepsilon = 0.5$ the window round 0 already holds $\tfrac19, \tfrac1{10}, \dots$). The bound $P(\text{cover}) \le \sum_k \varepsilon/2^k$ is countable subadditivity, which the `explain` line of the same screen states correctly; the hint still teaches the wrong tool for overlapping sets. | Williams, *Probability with Martingales*, 1.9 (monotonicity and countable subadditivity are separate lemmas; additivity needs disjointness); Folland, *Real Analysis*, Thm 1.8(c). `measure-prob-L1-concepts.py`, check `l1-cover-overlap` (EXPECTED_FAIL: the cover intervals overlap at $\varepsilon = 0.5, 0.05, 0.01$). | `hints[1].text`: "Use monotonicity, then countable subadditivity: the cover's chance is at most the sum of its lengths, overlaps or not."

2. app/content/courses/measure-prob/l4-measurable-functions.json | summary.ideas[1] | imprecise | "A function is measurable against a smaller sigma-algebra when it is constant on its atoms." True for a sigma-algebra generated by a finite partition (the lesson's $\sigma(\text{first die})$, and every coin-toss $\mathcal{F}_n$ of level 3), which is the only case the lesson uses, but false as a general rule and the summary states it generally. The atoms of the Borel sets on $[0, 1]$ are the points, so every function is constant on them, and $\mathbf{1}_V$ for the Vitali set of lesson 1 is not Borel measurable (lesson 2 says $V$ is not Borel). A learner who carries "constant on atoms" to the Borel sets, or to the Brownian filtrations that level 3 lists as prerequisites (ito L2), has a false test of measurability. | Measurability for a finite partition: Williams 3.13 (Doob's lemma) specialised to a finite $\sigma(Y)$; Shreve, *Stochastic Calculus for Finance I*, 2.1. Counterexample: Folland 1.1 (Vitali) with B([0,1]) whose atoms are singletons. | `summary.ideas[1]`: "Against a sigma-algebra with finitely many atoms, a function is measurable exactly when it is constant on each atom. The distribution function is right-continuous, with left limit $P(X < x)$."

## Level 2

3. app/content/courses/measure-prob/l8-modes-of-convergence.json | screens[10].prose | imprecise | "The tree's $S_T$ converges to the lognormal in distribution. That settles every bounded payoff, and a call needs a check on the tails too." Convergence in distribution gives $E[g(S_T^{(n)})] \to E[g(S_T)]$ for bounded $g$ that is continuous except on a set the limit law gives chance 0. A digital's single jump at $K$ passes (the lognormal gives $\{K\}$ chance 0), so every payoff the course meets is fine, but "every bounded payoff" drops the continuity condition and a learner carries away "bounded is enough". | Billingsley, *Probability and Measure*, Thm 25.7 (mapping theorem: $h$ continuous off a set of $P$-measure 0) and Thm 25.8 (bounded continuous $g$); Billingsley, *Convergence of Probability Measures*, Thm 2.1 (portmanteau). The earlier read fixed the unbounded-call side of this sentence (its warning 9); the bounded side is the remaining gap. | `screens[10].prose`: "The binomial tree for the course option, at even step counts, prices £10.4306 at 100 steps and £10.4466 at 500. Black-Scholes gives £10.4506. The tree's $S_T$ converges to the lognormal in distribution. That settles every bounded payoff whose jumps the lognormal gives chance 0, such as a digital, and a call needs a check on the tails too."

4. app/content/courses/measure-prob/l8-modes-of-convergence.json | screens[10].explain.text | imprecise | "A backtest's average return converges almost surely (strong law), and its scaled error in distribution (limits-thm l11)." Stated as a fact about backtests with no hypothesis. The strong law needs independent, identically distributed (or stationary ergodic) returns with a finite mean; the central limit theorem needs a finite variance. Real backtest returns are autocorrelated and heavy-tailed, which is exactly where a learner would misapply both. | Durrett, *Probability: Theory and Examples*, Thm 2.4.1 (SLLN: iid, $E|X_1| < \infty$), Thm 3.4.1 (CLT: iid, finite variance); Williams 12.10 (SLLN). | `screens[10].explain.text`: "$0.0200/0.0040 = 5$, the ratio of the step counts, and the tree price $E[g(S_T)]$ needs only the law of $S_T$, so convergence in distribution is the main step. For independent, identically distributed returns with a finite mean, a backtest's average converges almost surely (strong law). With a finite variance its scaled error converges in distribution (limits-thm l11)."
