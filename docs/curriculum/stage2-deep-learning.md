### 0.3 Conventions for every lesson

**Ids** follow `CONTENT-FORMAT.md` 4.1. Topic `top.deep-learning`; library namespace `dl` (`skl.dl.*`, `mc.dl.*`, `gen.dl.*`, `drl.dl.*`, `itm.dl.*`), which no sibling uses. Courses `crs.deepnets` (DN), `crs.autodiff` (AD), `crs.dltrain` (TD), `crs.convres` (CR), `crs.transformers` (TF); levels `lvl.<course>.<slug>`, lessons `lsn.<course>.<slug>`, homework `set.<course>.<level-slug>`, topic exam `exm.dl`, the Lab project `prj.dl.lab`. The `#` column is the lesson's number within 2.1b (1 to 80); display numbers are computed from position and never stored.

**Every lesson** runs the design research 8.3 sequence: a hook on a canvas, free manipulation, the name for what the learner just did, a ladder of 2 to 4 screens adding one constraint each, the trap, the rule stated as a description of the learner's own steps with at least one derived line the learner completes, a transfer screen where a true quant or ML context exists, the summary card, then a quiz of 3 to 5 items. From the owner's skills, every lesson also carries the "why this exists" frame, one common trap, at least one edge case, a worked example with a decision point where the naive route fails, and a motivated leap wherever a creative step appears (`derive` screens carry `leap: naive, fails, pointsTo`). In deep learning these take particular forms:

- **Manipulation before notation** means stepping a tape forward and then backward, dragging a kernel along a signal, sliding a temperature until a softmax flattens, toggling a residual connection and watching a gradient survive, or masking a cell of an attention grid, before any formula appears.
- **The worked example's decision point** is usually a choice between two procedures that both look right: sweep backwards in tape order or in reverse; `+=` or `np.add.at`; batch or layer statistics; mask before or after the softmax; the largest model or the compute-optimal one; the paper's per-token loss or the per-character one.
- **Derive** means completing a line of a backward pass, a shape of a vector-Jacobian product, a variance through a layer, an output-length formula, or a parameter count.
- **Shapes on screen.** Every array on a lesson screen shows its shape, and every gradient its parameter's shape, before any value [our choice]: shape errors are the commonest engine bug, and a learner who checks shapes first finds most of them.

**Widgets** use the ids of `docs/WIDGETS.md`: the ML composites of its section 5.17 (`nn-playground`, `relu-composer`, `sequence-filter`, `loss-lab`, `logistic-lab`, `calibration-lab`, `error-curves`, `fit-explorer`, `ensemble-lab`, `time-cv-lab`, `cv-lab`), the calculus and linear algebra composites (`descent-lab`, `dependency-graph`, `surface-explorer`, `matrix-multiply`, `pca-lab`), the programming composites (`predict-output`, `code-tracer`, `float-inspector`, `ndarray-lab`, `broadcast-lab`), the probability and Stage 0 composites (`dist-explorer`, `mass-editor`, `contingency-table`, `pascal-triangle`), and the shared inputs (`plot-2d`, `slider`, `stepper`, `value-table`, `live-formula`, `matrix`, `choice`, `numeric-input`, `expression-input`, `derivation-steps`, `card-sort`, `free-response`, `code-cell` in homework only). Seven new widgets carry ideas no existing widget shows: `autodiff-tape`, `training-curves`, `conv-lab`, `attention-lab`, `lm-sampler`, `tokeniser-lab` and `scaling-plot` (section 8). A mode a widget does not yet list is marked [mode to add]; section 8.2 collects them.

**Bertie** follows decision 12. In widget screens he reads the widget's `describe()` and, in lesson and explore modes only, its `secret()` block (planted truth, exact adjoints, the fixture's hidden curve) under the never-reveal instruction. He may offer a "Try this" chip that sets a slider or steps a tape, which the learner taps. Guidance he prefers to statements: "which node uses this value?", "what shape must this gradient have?", "what would the loss be if the model knew nothing?"

**Quiz item types** (`CONTENT-FORMAT.md` 3.4), the letters of the siblings: N numeric, X expression, C single choice, M multi-select, O order the steps, S sort or match, I interval, P point or curve on a canvas, R region selection, A matrix or vector entry, T predict the output of shown code. K (code with tests) appears only in homework and the Lab (decision 5). Long-form items appear in homework and exams, never in quizzes. **Production items** (`ASSESSMENT.md` 2.3): the 12 lessons whose objective begins Derive or Show (7, 14, 18, 21, 22, 23, 36, 48, 56, 61, 65, 67) each add one short production item, a 2 to 4 line derivation or a two-sentence explanation in `free-response` marked against a 3-point rubric, as a fifth quiz item; their O items become "write the missing step" items. The counts in section 0.4 exclude these 12 items. Single and multiple choice stay at or below 40% of each course's quiz items (design research 10); section 0.4 counts them.

**Skills.** Each level lists its skills at level grain (`ASSESSMENT.md` 4.1): 5 to 7 a level, each introduced by one lesson, at most 2 a lesson; finer distinctions (the $\frac1B$ of a batch, the floor in an output length) are step tags. Skill ids are `skl.dl.<verb-phrase>`.

**Misconceptions.** Every `mc.dl.*` id in the tables is new [new] and joins the register. Where an item meets an error 2.1 already registered (`mc.ml.gradient-from-one-path` for a fan-out missed in a derivation, `mc.ml.zero-initialisation-is-fine`, `mc.ml.high-learning-rate-trains-faster`), its author uses the 2.1 id rather than a new one; the tables name only the new errors each lesson's trap screen is built on.

**Level review.** Every level ends with a Level review of 8 items at difficulty 2 to 4, interleaved, with up to 30% drawn from earlier levels of the same course (`ASSESSMENT.md` 2.4). The homework set follows (section 3).

**Columns.** Objective is one measurable sentence. Hook is the puzzle the lesson opens with, with its answer in brackets where it is a number. Trap is the misconception the trap screen is built around; Edge is the fringe case the lesson must show. Quiz lists the item types in order.

## 1. Item overview

### 1.1 Purpose in a quant or ML career

- **It is what ML labs test.** A research interview at a frontier lab expects a candidate to write backpropagation for an arbitrary layer, explain why attention is scaled and masked, predict a model's loss at initialisation, and say why a deep residual network trains when a plain one does not. 2.1 gives the first pass at two levels; 2.1b gives the working knowledge, built by hand. Without it the curriculum produces a strong quant who has used a neural network but never built one (canon-gap G14's verdict).
- **Building the engine is the understanding.** Automatic differentiation, the tape, vector-Jacobian products and fused stable operations are the machinery every framework hides. A learner who has written them reads PyTorch or JAX code as a description of a tape and debugs it by its shapes (course AD).
- **Training is an experimental science.** Most of a deep-learning researcher's week is reading curves: initial loss, saturation, gradient norms, update ratios, train against validation. Course TD teaches these as measurable habits, and the exit holds the learner to a reference within 0.05 nats.
- **Quant work uses it where the data are sequences, images or text.** Tabular returns favour trees (2.1 lesson 116), but order-book event streams, limit-order-book "images", news and filings are sequence and text problems where convolutional and attention models earn their place (lessons 54, 57 and 80). Risk systems compute many sensitivities at once by reverse-mode differentiation, the same algorithm as backpropagation (Giles and Glasserman 2006, lesson 19's transfer).
- **Honest evaluation transfers.** Perplexities across tokenisers, test-set contamination and the sampling error of a validation loss (lesson 79) are 2.1's leakage lessons in a new setting; the 3.1 option asks how much of double descent survives a tuned penalty.
- **Compute literacy.** The $6ND$ rule and the compute-optimal ratio (lessons 28 and 78) let a learner size any training run, from the half-hour browser exit to a lab's budget, and say what a scaling claim does and does not promise.

### Course DN: Deep Networks: Functions, Losses and Fitting (`crs.deepnets`, 3 levels, 16 lessons)

What a deep network computes, what it should be trained to minimise, and how it is fitted when the loss is not convex. 2.1 showed that ReLU sums bend and that depth multiplies pieces; this course counts regions in the plane, reads a network as a different linear map in each region, composes networks by folding, prices width against depth for a fixed budget, derives every loss from a distribution over outputs (including a network that predicts its own uncertainty), computes cross-entropy without overflow, calibrates confidence, and adds the fitting tools 2.1 and 2.0b left out: schedules, the learning-rate sweep, decoupled weight decay, clipping and the symmetries of a network's loss. Spine: Prince chapters 3 to 6 [searched: titles]; 6.7960's lecture on training [searched: lecture 2 slides]; Montúfar and others (2014); Guo, Pleiss, Sun and Weinberger (2017); Loshchilov and Hutter (2019).

### Course AD: Automatic Differentiation from Scratch (`crs.autodiff`, 3 levels, 17 lessons)

How a program computes the gradient of anything it computes. 1.3 lesson 116 applied the chain rule to a small graph by hand and 2.1 lesson 111 did it for a two-layer network; this course turns the chain rule into an algorithm: record the computation as a tape, sweep it backwards, add contributions where a value is used twice, pick reverse mode because a loss has one output, write each operation's vector-Jacobian product from its shapes, and sum gradients over broadcast axes. It then derives, by hand, every gradient a character language model needs (Karpathy's "Becoming a Backprop Ninja" [searched: title], rebuilt here in NumPy) and builds the engine the rest of 2.1b trains with. Spine: Prince 7.1 to 7.4 [searched: section titles]; Karpathy's micrograd video [searched]; 6.7960's backpropagation lecture [searched: lecture 2 covers computation graphs, backpropagation through chains, MLPs and DAGs, and differentiable programming]; Baydin, Pearlmutter, Radul and Siskind (2018); Griewank and Walther (2008) as reference.

### Course CR: Convolution and Residual Networks (`crs.convres`, 2 levels, 13 lessons)

The two architectural ideas that made depth work before transformers and still sit inside them. Convolution shares one small set of weights across positions, which buys equivariance and a parameter count independent of input length; its arithmetic (padding, stride, dilation, channels, receptive fields, pooling) and its backward pass are computed by hand and implemented as a matrix product. Residual connections give the gradient an identity path, at the price of a stream whose variance grows, and turn a deep network into an ensemble of shallow paths. The course builds Karpathy's hierarchical character model and the item's small CNN. Spine: Prince 10 [check sections] and 11 [searched: 11.1, 11.3 to 11.5]; Karpathy's makemore part 5 [searched: "WaveNet", 56 minutes]; van den Oord and others (2016); He, Zhang, Ren and Sun (2016); Veit, Wilber and Belongie (2016); Greydanus (2020) for MNIST-1D.

- *Example A (long-form, d4).* (a) Prove that $D$ lines in general position (no two parallel, no three through one point) divide the plane into $1 + D + \binom D2$ regions. (b) Explain why a shallow ReLU network with two inputs and $D$ hidden units is linear on each region, and why its number of linear regions is at most this. (c) Give the count for $D = 5$ and say what happens when three switching lines meet in one point.
  *Model answer.* (1) With no lines there is 1 region. (2) Add the lines one at a time. The $k$-th line meets each of the $k - 1$ earlier lines exactly once, at distinct points, because no two are parallel and no three are concurrent. (3) Those $k - 1$ points cut the new line into $k$ pieces, and each piece splits one existing region in two, so the $k$-th line adds exactly $k$ regions. (4) The total is $1 + \sum_{k=1}^Dk = 1 + \frac{D(D + 1)}2 = 1 + D + \binom D2$. (5) Hidden unit $j$ computes $\max(0, \mathbf w_j\cdot\mathbf x + b_j)$, which is linear on each side of the line $\mathbf w_j\cdot\mathbf x + b_j = 0$. (6) Inside one region of the arrangement every unit keeps its on-or-off state, so the output, a fixed linear combination of the active units' affine functions, is affine there (lesson 2). (7) Two neighbouring regions may happen to share a formula, so the number of distinct linear pieces is at most the number of regions. (8) For $D = 5$: $1 + 5 + 10 = 16$. Three lines through one point lose the small triangle they would have enclosed: one region fewer.
  *Rubric (10).* 1: the base case (line 1). 3: the $k$-th line meets $k - 1$ lines at distinct points and adds $k$ regions, with general position used (lines 2, 3), the creative step. 1: the sum (line 4). 2: linearity of each unit on each side of its line (line 5). 2: the output is affine on each region and the count is an upper bound (lines 6, 7). 1: $D = 5$ and the concurrent case (line 8).
- *Example B (d5).* The tent map $t(x) = \lvert 2x - 1\rvert$ sends $[0, 1]$ onto $[0, 1]$ in two pieces; lesson 3's zigzag $g$ does so in three. Let $h = g\circ t\circ t\circ t$ on $[0, 1]$. (a) How many linear pieces has $h$? (b) What is the fewest hidden units a single-hidden-layer ReLU network needs to represent $h$ on $[0, 1]$? (c) Build $h$ as a deep network with hidden layers of widths 2, 2, 2 and 3 and count its parameters; count the shallow network's.
  *Answer.* (a) $t\circ t\circ t$ has $2^3 = 8$ pieces, each sweeping $[0, 1]$ once, so composing with $g$ copies $g$'s 3 pieces into each: 24 pieces [verified in exact rational arithmetic]. (b) 24 pieces have 23 interior kinks, and one hidden layer gives at most one kink per unit (2.1 lesson 109), so at least 23 units. 23 are not enough: with exactly 23, every unit sits at one kink, and a unit's slope on the first piece is either 0 (it switches on to the right of its kink) or minus its slope jump (it is on to the left). $h$'s slopes alternate $\pm 24$, so every jump is $\pm 48$ and the first piece's slope, $-24$, would have to be a sum of multiples of 48, which it is not. A 24th unit that is on across all of $[0, 1]$, such as $\operatorname{ReLU}(x)$, supplies the first slope: 24 units [verified: exact rational slopes, and a least-squares fit with 24 units exact to $10^{-13}$]. (c) $t(x) = \operatorname{ReLU}(2x - 1) + \operatorname{ReLU}(1 - 2x)$ uses 2 units, and $g$ uses 3. Parameters: $(1\times 2 + 2) + 2\times(2\times 2 + 2) + (2\times 3 + 3) + (3\times 1 + 1) = 4 + 12 + 9 + 4 = 29$, against $3\times 24 + 1 = 73$ for the shallow network. Decision point: the naive count adds pieces across layers ($2 + 2 + 2 + 3 = 9$) and concludes depth buys little; the pieces multiply because every piece of the inner map sweeps the outer map's whole input.

**DN2 Losses from likelihood** (`set.deepnets.losses`). 8 items (3 d3, 3 d4, 2 d5). Long-form: the heteroscedastic Gaussian loss and its collapse. - *Example A (long-form, d4).* A network outputs a mean $\mu$ and $s = \ln\sigma$ for a target $y$, trained with the Gaussian negative log-likelihood. (a) Write the loss for one example. (b) Derive its gradients with respect to $\mu$ and $s$. (c) Show the loss is minimised over $\sigma$ at $\sigma = \lvert y - \mu\rvert$ and give the minimum. (d) Explain what goes wrong if the network can fit some training points exactly, and give two remedies.
  *Model answer.* (1) The Gaussian density gives $\ell = \frac{(y - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} + \ln\sigma + \frac12\ln 2\pi = \frac{r^2}2e^{-2s} + s + \frac12\ln 2\pi$ with $r = y - \mu$. (2) $\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\mu} = -\frac{r}{\sigma^2}$: the squared-error gradient divided by the predicted variance, so confident predictions are pushed hardest. (3) $\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial s} = 1 - r^2e^{-2s} = 1 - \frac{r^2}{\sigma^2}$. (4) Setting it to zero gives $\sigma^2 = r^2$; the second derivative $2r^2e^{-2s} > 0$ confirms a minimum. (5) The minimum is $\frac12 + \ln\lvert r\rvert + \frac12\ln 2\pi$. (6) Predicting $s$ rather than $\sigma$ keeps $\sigma = e^s$ positive without a constraint. (7) If the network fits a point exactly, $r = 0$, line 5 gives $\ln 0 = -\infty$: the training loss falls without bound by shrinking that point's $\sigma$, and the gradient in line 2 explodes as $\sigma\to 0$. (8) Remedies: a floor $\sigma\ge\sigma_{\min}$ (or $\sigma = \operatorname{softplus}(\cdot) + \sigma_{\min}$), a penalty on $s$, or early stopping on held-out likelihood, where exact fits do not repeat.
  *Rubric (10).* 2: the loss with its constants (line 1). 2: both gradients (lines 2, 3). 2: the minimiser with the second-order check and the minimum value (lines 4, 5). 1: why predict $\ln\sigma$ (line 6). 2: the collapse, with the gradient's behaviour (line 7). 1: two remedies (line 8).
- *Example B (d5).* A two-class network's held-out logit margins (true class minus the other) are 4, 4, 4 and $-4$: three confident correct predictions and one confident wrong one. (a) Compute the mean held-out loss at temperatures 1, 2, 4 and 8. (b) Find the temperature that minimises it exactly. (c) Give the calibrated confidence and compare it with the accuracy. (d) Has the accuracy changed?
  *Answer.* (a) With margin $m$ the loss is $\ln(1 + e^{-m/T})$. Mean losses: $T = 1$: 1.018; $T = 2$: 0.627; $T = 4$: 0.563; $T = 8$: 0.599. (b) Write $u = \frac4T$. The mean loss is $\frac14[3\ln(1 + e^{-u}) + \ln(1 + e^u)] = \frac14[4\ln(1 + e^{-u}) + u]$, using $\ln(1 + e^u) = u + \ln(1 + e^{-u})$. Its derivative in $u$ is $\frac14\left[1 - \frac{4}{1 + e^u}\right]$, zero when $e^u = 3$: $u = \ln 3$, so $T = \frac4{\ln 3} = 3.64$, with mean loss 0.562. (c) The calibrated confidence is $\sigma(u) = \frac{3}{4}$, exactly the accuracy, 3 of 4. (d) No: dividing every margin by $T > 0$ keeps every sign. Decision point: the naive reading takes the confident wrong prediction as a reason to retrain; one temperature fitted on held-out data fixes the overconfidence without touching the ranking, and the optimum lands where confidence equals accuracy.

- *Example A (long-form, d4).* (a) Write one step of Adam with an $L_2$ penalty $\frac\lambda2\Vert\mathbf w\Vert^2$ added to the loss, and one step of AdamW. (b) In steady state, estimate the per-step shrinkage the penalty causes for weight A, whose data gradient has mean 0 and RMS 10, and weight B, whose data gradient has mean 0 and RMS 0.01, with $w = 1$ and $\lambda = 0.01$ in both. (c) Compare with AdamW and say which behaviour is intended.
  *Model answer.* (1) Adam with $L_2$: $\mathbf g' = \nabla L + \lambda\mathbf w$; $\mathbf m\leftarrow\beta_1\mathbf m + (1 - \beta_1)\mathbf g'$; $\mathbf v\leftarrow\beta_2\mathbf v + (1 - \beta_2)\mathbf g'^2$; $\mathbf w\leftarrow\mathbf w - \eta\frac{\hat{\mathbf m}}{\sqrt{\hat{\mathbf v}} + \epsilon}$. (2) AdamW: the same moments of $\nabla L$ alone, then $\mathbf w\leftarrow\mathbf w - \eta\frac{\hat{\mathbf m}}{\sqrt{\hat{\mathbf v}} + \epsilon} - \eta\lambda\mathbf w$. (3) The leap: in steady state $\hat{\mathbf m}\approx E[\mathbf g']$ and $\sqrt{\hat{\mathbf v}}\approx\operatorname{RMS}(\mathbf g')$, so the penalty's share of the step is $\eta\frac{\lambda w}{\operatorname{RMS}(g')}$: the decay is divided by each weight's own gradient scale. (4) Weight A: $\operatorname{RMS}(g')\approx 10$, so the shrinkage is $\eta\times\frac{0.01}{10} = 0.001\eta$ a step. (5) Weight B: $g'$ has mean $\lambda w = 0.01$ and RMS $\sqrt{0.01^2 + 0.01^2} = 0.0141$, so the shrinkage is $\eta\times\frac{0.01}{0.0141} = 0.71\eta$ a step, about 700 times A's. (6) AdamW shrinks both by $\eta\lambda w = 0.01\eta$ a step. (7) Weight decay is meant to pull every weight towards zero at the same relative rate, as the $L_2$ prior says; with $L_2$ inside Adam, weights with large gradients (often the important ones) are barely regularised and quiet ones are crushed. - *Example A (long-form, d4).* A one-dimensional convolution (cross-correlation) with kernel $\mathbf w$ of length $k$ computes $y_t = \sum_{j=0}^{k-1}w_jx_{t+j}$ for $t = 0, \dots, L - k$. (a) Let $(S\mathbf x)_t = x_{t-1}$ shift the input right. Prove that $\operatorname{conv}(S\mathbf x) = S\operatorname{conv}(\mathbf x)$ away from the boundary, and say what happens at it. (b) Derive $\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_j}$ and $\frac{\partial L}{\partial x_s}$ given $\bar y_t$. (c) Explain in one sentence each why a CNN on images is equivariant to translation but a CNN with pooling is only approximately invariant.
  *Model answer.* (1) $\operatorname{conv}(S\mathbf x)_t = \sum_jw_j(S\mathbf x)_{t+j} = \sum_jw_jx_{t+j-1} = \operatorname{conv}(\mathbf x)_{t-1} = (S\operatorname{conv}(\mathbf x))_t$. (2) The identity needs $x_{t+j-1}$ to exist for every $j$; at the left edge the shifted input brings in a padded value, so the equality fails within $k - 1$ positions of the boundary, and circular padding would make it exact. (3) Each $w_j$ is used at every output position, so its gradient sums over them: $\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_j} = \sum_t\bar y_tx_{t+j}$, the correlation of the input with the upstream gradient. - *Example A (long-form, d4).* A network has $K$ residual blocks $\mathbf x_{k+1} = \mathbf x_k + F_k(\mathbf x_k)$. (a) Write $\frac{\partial\mathbf x_K}{\partial\mathbf x_0}$ as a product and expand it as a sum over paths. (b) Explain, with a scalar example, why the gradient need not vanish however deep the network is, and why it could explode. (c) Show that independent branch outputs of variance $v$ make the stream's variance grow like $1 + Kv$, and give two remedies. (d) Explain the degradation result of lesson 59 in these terms.
  *Model answer.* (1) $\frac{\partial\mathbf x_K}{\partial\mathbf x_0} = \prod_{k=K-1}^{0}\left(\mathbf I + \mathbf J_k\right)$ with $\mathbf J_k = \frac{\partial F_k}{\partial\mathbf x_k}$. (2) Expanding the product picks, for each block, either $\mathbf I$ or $\mathbf J_k$: a sum of $2^K$ terms, one per path, the path through no branches being $\mathbf I$. (3) Scalar case with every $J_k = j$: the factor is $(1 + j)^K$; a plain network gives $j^K$. With $j = 0.05$ and $K = 10$, $(1.05)^{10} = 1.63$ against $0.05^{10}\approx 10^{-13}$. (4) The identity term guarantees a gradient of order 1 reaches early layers whatever the branches do; but $(1 + j)^K$ grows if every $j > 0$, so branches must stay small or be normalised. (5) If branch outputs are independent of the stream with variance $v$, variances add: $\operatorname{Var}(\mathbf x_{k+1}) = \operatorname{Var}(\mathbf x_k) + v$, so after $K$ blocks $1 + Kv$. (6) Remedies: scale each branch by $\frac1{\sqrt K}$, giving $1 + v$; or pre-norm, normalising each branch's input so its output scale does not grow with the stream (Xiong and others 2020), with a final normalisation. (7) A plain deep network must learn identity maps to do as well as a shallow one, and its gradient is a product of many factors that the optimiser cannot keep near 1; a residual network starts near the identity, so the deep network is at least as easy to train as the shallow one.
  *Rubric (10).* 1: the product (line 1). 2: the sum over paths (line 2). 2: the scalar comparison (line 3). 1: why it can still explode (line 4). 2: variance growth with a remedy (lines 5, 6). 2: degradation explained (line 7).

- *Example A (long-form, d4).* (a) For $\mathbf q, \mathbf k\in\mathbb R^{d_k}$ with independent entries of mean 0 and variance 1, show $\operatorname{Var}(\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf k) = d_k$. (b) Using the softmax Jacobian $\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf p) - \mathbf p\mathbf p^\top$, explain why large scores stall learning, and why dividing by $\sqrt{d_k}$ helps. (c) Show that setting masked scores to $-\infty$ before the softmax keeps each row a probability distribution over the allowed positions, and that zeroing after it does not.
  *Model answer.* (1) $\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf k = \sum_iq_ik_i$; each term has mean $E[q_i]E[k_i] = 0$ and variance $E[q_i^2]E[k_i^2] = 1$ by independence. (2) The terms are independent, so variances add: $d_k$. (3) With scores of standard deviation $\sqrt{d_k}$ (8 for $d_k = 64$), the largest score in a row typically exceeds the next by several units, so the softmax is nearly one-hot. (4) The Jacobian's entries are $p_i(\delta_{ij} - p_j)$; when one $p_i\approx 1$ and the rest $\approx 0$, every entry is close to 0, so almost no gradient reaches the scores and the queries and keys stop learning. (5) Dividing by $\sqrt{d_k}$ gives scores of unit variance whatever the head size, keeping the softmax in its responsive range at initialisation. (6) With $-\infty$ at masked positions, $e^{-\infty} = 0$, so those positions get weight 0 and the remaining weights are the softmax of the allowed scores: positive and summing to 1. (7) Zeroing after the softmax removes mass the allowed positions had, so the row sums to less than 1 and depends on the future scores through the normaliser: the future still leaks into the past.
  *Rubric (10).* 3: the variance, with independence used twice (lines 1, 2). 3: saturation through the Jacobian and the fix (lines 3 to 5). 2: masking before the softmax (line 6). 2: why masking after fails, including the leak (line 7).

- *Example A (long-form, d4).* A two-layer attention-only transformer predicts the next token of sequences such as $\dots, A, B, \dots, A, ?$ by copying $B$. (a) Describe what a previous-token head in layer 1 writes into each position's residual stream. (b) Describe how an induction head in layer 2 uses it: what its query and keys must match, and what its value copies. (c) Explain why one layer cannot do this. (d) Say how you would detect such a head in a trained model, and one caution.
  *Model answer.* (1) A previous-token head at position $t$ attends to position $t - 1$ and writes (a projection of) the previous token's identity into position $t$'s stream: each position now carries "my token" and "the token before me". (3) Each earlier position's key encodes "the token before me", from layer 1; the key at the position holding $B$ says "the token before me is $A$", so it matches the query. ### 4.2 The three exit slots in full (specimen versions)

**Q18 (specimen, d5): the backward pass of causal self-attention.** "One head computes $\mathbf Q = \mathbf X\mathbf W_q$, $\mathbf K = \mathbf X\mathbf W_k$, $\mathbf V = \mathbf X\mathbf W_v$ ($\mathbf X$ is $T\times d$), scores $\mathbf S = \frac{\mathbf Q\mathbf K^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}} + \mathbf M$ with $M_{ij} = -\infty$ for $j > i$ and 0 otherwise, weights $\mathbf A = \operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf S)$ row by row, and output $\mathbf Y = \mathbf A\mathbf V$. Given $\bar{\mathbf Y}$, derive $\bar{\mathbf V}$, $\bar{\mathbf A}$, $\bar{\mathbf S}$, $\bar{\mathbf Q}$, $\bar{\mathbf K}$, $\bar{\mathbf W}_q$ and $\bar{\mathbf X}$, state each shape, and say how you would check the result."

*Model answer.* (1) $\mathbf Y = \mathbf A\mathbf V$ is a matrix product (lesson 21): $\bar{\mathbf V} = \mathbf A^\top\bar{\mathbf Y}$ ($T\times d_v$) and $\bar{\mathbf A} = \bar{\mathbf Y}\mathbf V^\top$ ($T\times T$). (2) Each row of $\mathbf A$ is the softmax of the same row of $\mathbf S$, with Jacobian $\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf a) - \mathbf a\mathbf a^\top$, so row by row $\bar{\mathbf s}_i = \mathbf a_i\odot\left(\bar{\mathbf a}_i - (\bar{\mathbf a}_i\cdot\mathbf a_i)\mathbf 1\right)$; in matrix form $\bar{\mathbf S} = \mathbf A\odot\left(\bar{\mathbf A} - \operatorname{rowsum}(\bar{\mathbf A}\odot\mathbf A)\right)$ ($T\times T$). (3) Masked entries have $A_{ij} = 0$, so $\bar S_{ij} = 0$ there: no gradient flows to the future, and the mask itself needs no backward rule. (4) $\mathbf S = \frac{1}{\sqrt{d_k}}\mathbf Q\mathbf K^\top$ gives $\bar{\mathbf Q} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{d_k}}\bar{\mathbf S}\mathbf K$ ($T\times d_k$) and $\bar{\mathbf K} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{d_k}}\bar{\mathbf S}^\top\mathbf Q$ ($T\times d_k$). (5) $\mathbf Q = \mathbf X\mathbf W_q$ gives $\bar{\mathbf W}_q = \mathbf X^\top\bar{\mathbf Q}$ ($d\times d_k$), and likewise for $\mathbf W_k$ and $\mathbf W_v$. (6) $\mathbf X$ is used three times, so its gradient is the sum of three contributions (lesson 18): $\bar{\mathbf X} = \bar{\mathbf Q}\mathbf W_q^\top + \bar{\mathbf K}\mathbf W_k^\top + \bar{\mathbf V}\mathbf W_v^\top$ ($T\times d$). (7) Check: every gradient has its variable's shape; each row of $\bar{\mathbf S}$ sums to zero (softmax ignores a shift); a central-difference check on a few entries of each parameter at relative $10^{-6}$ in float64 with $T = 4$, $d = 3$, $d_k = 2$, non-square so a transpose shows.
*Rubric (10).* 2: $\bar{\mathbf V}$ and $\bar{\mathbf A}$ with shapes (line 1). 3: the softmax backward pass, row by row or in matrix form (line 2). 1: the mask (line 3). 1: $\bar{\mathbf Q}$ and $\bar{\mathbf K}$ with the scale (line 4). 1: the weight gradient (line 5). 1: the three-way sum for $\bar{\mathbf X}$ (line 6). 1: the check (line 7). Common losses: the softmax treated elementwise ($\bar{\mathbf S} = \bar{\mathbf A}\odot\mathbf A(1 - \mathbf A)$, missing the off-diagonal terms); $\bar{\mathbf X}$ from one path only; the $\frac1{\sqrt{d_k}}$ dropped.

**Q19 (specimen, d3): why deep networks train.** "A colleague's 30-layer tanh network starts at a loss of 41 on a 10-class problem and its loss barely moves. Explain what is probably wrong and how initialisation, normalisation and residual connections each fix part of it. Give the loss you expect at initialisation and one measurement you would make to confirm each diagnosis."

*Model answer.* (1) A well-initialised 10-class classifier starts near $\ln 10 = 2.30$; 41 means confident, wrong logits, almost certainly an output layer initialised too large (lesson 40). (2) A loss that barely moves in a deep tanh stack suggests saturated units or vanishing gradients: if pre-activations have a large spread, most $\lvert\tanh\rvert$ values sit near 1 and pass gradients near 0 (lessons 24, 41). (3) Initialisation: draw weights with standard deviation $\frac{g}{\sqrt{n_{\text{in}}}}$ with the tanh gain $\frac53$, so activations keep a stable variance through depth; balance backward variance with $n_{\text{out}}$ or Glorot's compromise (lesson 42); shrink the output layer so the initial loss is near $\ln 10$. (4) Normalisation: batch or layer normalisation resets each layer's pre-activations to unit scale, so a bad scale cannot compound over 30 layers (lessons 43, 44). (5) Residual connections: $\mathbf x + F(\mathbf x)$ gives the gradient an identity path, so it reaches early layers even if the branches' Jacobians are small (lesson 60); with pre-norm the stream's growing variance is controlled (lesson 61). (6) Measurements: the first logged loss against $\ln 10$; histograms of each layer's activations (saturated share) and gradients (norms by depth); the update-to-data ratio per layer (lesson 45).
*Rubric (10).* 2: the expected initial loss and the diagnosis of 41 (line 1). 2: saturation or vanishing gradients, with the mechanism (line 2). 2: initialisation (line 3). 1: normalisation (line 4). 2: residual connections, with the identity path (line 5). 1: a measurement for each diagnosis (line 6). Common losses: "use ReLU" as the only fix; no expected initial loss; normalisation described without saying what it normalises over.

**Q20 (specimen, d3): attention and the transformer block.** "Explain how one pre-norm transformer block turns a sequence of $T$ vectors of width $d$ into another. Say why attention scores are divided by $\sqrt{d_k}$, why a causal mask is applied before the softmax, why positions must be added, what several heads buy, and where the block's parameters and its computation go."

*Model answer.* (1) Each vector is normalised, projected to a query, a key and a value per head; each position's output is a softmax-weighted average of values, weighted by how well its query matches each key (lesson 64). (2) The scores are divided by $\sqrt{d_k}$ because a dot product of $d_k$ unit-variance entries has variance $d_k$; unscaled scores saturate the softmax and stall learning (lesson 65). (3) The causal mask sets future scores to $-\infty$ before the softmax so each position averages only over the past and the rows still sum to 1; masking after the softmax leaks the future through the normaliser (lesson 66). (4) Without positions attention is permutation-equivariant and cannot tell "dog bites man" from "man bites dog"; learned or sinusoidal position embeddings break the symmetry (lesson 67). (5) Several heads let different positions be attended for different reasons at no extra parameter cost, $4d^2$ in all (lesson 68). (6) The attention output is added to the residual stream; then an MLP of width $4d$ transforms each position separately and is added again (lesson 71). (7) Parameters: about $4d^2$ in attention and $8d^2$ in the MLP, $12d^2$ a block. Computation: $4Td^2$ for the projections and $8Td^2$ for the MLP, plus $2T^2d$ for attention's scores and weighted sum, which exceeds the projections once $T > 2d$ (lesson 69) and the projections and MLP together once $T > 6d$.
*Rubric (10).* 2: the mechanism (line 1). 2: the scale with its variance reason (line 2). 1: the mask before the softmax (line 3). 1: positions (line 4). 1: heads (line 5). 1: the block's two residual additions (line 6). 2: parameters and computation, including the $T^2$ term (line 7). Common losses: "attention finds the important words" with no mechanism; the scale explained as "normalisation"; the MLP omitted.

## 6. Verification approach

Every graded answer is computed twice, by two implementations that share no code (`CONTENT-FORMAT.md` 6.2): the item's generator or widget model in TypeScript (`@zq/models`, `ml-core`), and the Python verifier `zqverify` run in CI on CPython 3.12 with the pinned stack, written from the generator's plain-language `semantics`. Disagreement fails the build. Deep learning adds four rules of its own [our choice]:

1. **Three references for every gradient.** The NumPy reference implementation's gradients are checked against central differences in float64 and against a PyTorch CPU implementation in float64 on stored weights (CI only; open item 10). A screen's or item's gradient must agree with all three.
2. **Fixture runs, not live claims.** Every training curve, accuracy or loss shown on a lesson screen comes from a fixture run computed in CI by the NumPy reference on a stored seed, stored with its hash, and replayed by `training-curves`. CI re-runs each fixture and fails if a regenerated value drifts more than 4 standard errors (across 5 seeds) from the stored one.
3. **Tolerances earn their width.** Every stated tolerance (the exit's 0.05 nats, the Lab's 3 percentage points) must exceed 4 times the seed-to-seed standard deviation of the reference measured over 5 seeds on CPython and on Pyodide, and every known-wrong implementation must fall outside it. If a tolerance fails this, the configuration or the tolerance changes and this file is revised.
4. **Float32 claims are run in float32.** Every overflow, `nan` or cancellation claim is reproduced in NumPy float32 (and float16 where stated) in CI, so the lesson's number is the machine's, not a hand estimate.

## 7. Sample lesson in full: "A computation recorded as a tape"

**Why this lesson** (decision 13). Every network in 2.1b is trained by the engine this lesson starts. 1.3 lesson 116 applied the chain rule to a small graph by hand, and 2.1 lesson 111 wrote two-layer gradients in matrix form; neither said how a program does it for any computation it happens to run. This lesson gives the procedure a machine runs: record the computation, then sweep the record backwards once. It carries the ordering trap every learner meets (sweeping in the forward order), the fan-out rule that the commonest engine bug breaks, and the reason training uses reverse mode at all.

### 7.1 Lesson header

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Id | `lsn.autodiff.tape` (course AD, level AD1, lesson 17 of 2.1b) |
| Length | 12 screens (1 title, 1 explore, 1 summary, 9 tasks) and a quiz of 5 items; about 11 minutes of screens and 4 of quiz |

**Screen 5. Which way back?** (`trap`, id `order`, `trap: { mc: mc.dl.backward-same-order, bait: A }`)

- **Prose:** "To get every rate, the program walks the tape a second time, starting from $L$, and each row works out how much $L$ changes per unit of that row's value. A row can only do that once it knows how much $L$ changes per unit of every row that uses it."

- **Question:** "In which order must the second walk visit the rows?"
- **Answer input:** `choice`, four cards.
- **Correct:** B.

| Option | Correct? | Misconception | Feedback |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. Top to bottom, rows 1 to 7, the order the program ran (the bait) | no | `mc.dl.backward-same-order` | "Row 1 would go first, but $a$ is used by row 5, which has not reported yet: row 1 cannot know its rate. Every row must wait for the rows that use it, and those come later on the tape." |

**Screen 7. The last two rows, and why not just multiply out?** (`derive`, id `rule`, `leap: { naive, fails, pointsTo }`)

- **Prose:** "Row 5 is $e = a\times b$. Complete its two inputs with the same rule."
- **Widget:** `derivation-steps` in fill mode beside the tape:
  1. $\bar a = \bar e\times\frac{\partial e}{\partial a} = \bar e\times b = (-4)\times(-2) = \boxed{\phantom{0}}$
  2. $\bar b = \bar e\times\frac{\partial e}{\partial b} = \bar e\times a = (-4)\times 3 = \boxed{\phantom{0}}$
- **Leap** (shown after Check, as the worked example's decision point):
  - *Naive:* "Multiply the program out, $L = (ab + c)f = abf + cf$, and differentiate it once for each leaf: $\frac{\partial L}{\partial a} = bf = 8$ and $\frac{\partial L}{\partial b} = af = -12$. With four leaves this works."
  - *Fails:* "A network has millions of leaves, and its formula multiplied out would be astronomically long. Each leaf's derivative would repeat the same downstream part (here the factor $f$, reached through $d$) once per leaf."
  - *Points to:* "Compute the downstream part once, as the adjoint $\bar e$, store it on the tape, and reuse it for every input of $e$. Every row is visited once, whatever the number of leaves."
- **Answer input:** the two boxes, `numeric-input`.
- **Correct:** 8 and $-12$.

| Wrong answer | Misconception | Feedback |
|---|---|---|
| $\bar a = -2$, $\bar b = 3$ | `mc.dl.local-derivative-only` | "Those are the local derivatives $b$ and $a$. Multiply each by $\bar e = -4$." |
| $\bar a = -12$, $\bar b = 8$ | `mc.dl.swap-product-partials` | "Swapped: $a$'s local derivative in $e = ab$ is $b$, not $a$." |
| $\bar a = -8$ or $\bar b = 12$ | `mc.dl.sign-slip` | "Two negatives multiply to a positive: $(-4)\times(-2) = 8$." |
| anything else | (otherwise) | "Adjoint of row 5 times the local derivative for each input." |

- **Hints.** Nudge: "What does $e$ change by when $a$ moves by one?" Point: highlights $b$'s value. Step: "$\frac{\partial e}{\partial a} = b = -2$, so $\bar a = -4\times(-2) = 8$; $\frac{\partial e}{\partial b} = a = 3$, so $\bar b = -4\times 3 = -12$."
- **Why?** Yours: "$\bar a = 8$, which matches the nudge on screen 2, and $\bar b = -12$." Then: "The rule you used at every row: **an input's adjoint gets the row's adjoint times the row's local derivative**."
- **Tutor.** Focus: the rule, and why reuse beats multiplying out. Notes: learners who multiply out get the right numbers; ask how they would do it for a million leaves. Avoid: claiming the multiplied-out route is wrong; it is correct and does not scale.

**Screen 10. The rule, named** (`generalise`, id `named`)

- **Rule (prose):** "Run the computation forward, recording each operation, its inputs and its value on a tape. Set the output's adjoint to 1. Visit the rows in reverse order; at each row, add the row's adjoint times its local derivative into each input's adjoint. When the sweep ends, every input's adjoint is the derivative of the output with respect to it. This is **reverse-mode automatic differentiation**; in a network it is called backpropagation."
- **Tex:** $\bar v_i = \sum_{j\text{ uses }i}\bar v_j\,\frac{\partial v_j}{\partial v_i}$, with $\bar v_{\text{output}} = 1$.
- **Confirmation question:** "How many backward sweeps does the original tape need to give the derivatives of $L$ with respect to all four leaves?" (`choice`)
- **Correct:** A.

| Option | Correct? | Misconception | Feedback |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. One | yes | | "Yes: one sweep fills every adjoint." |
| B. Four, one per leaf | no | `mc.dl.one-sweep-per-input` | "That is forward mode's cost, one pass per input (lesson 19). The backward sweep gives every input's adjoint in a single pass." |
| C. Seven, one per row | no | (one pass per row) | "Each row is visited once within a single sweep." |
| D. Two: a forward one and a backward one for each leaf | no | `mc.dl.one-sweep-per-input` | "One forward pass and one backward sweep in total, not per leaf." |

- **Why?** Shown: "One forward pass and one backward sweep give all four derivatives: 8, $-12$, $-4$ and 1." Then: "That is why training uses reverse mode: a loss is one number that depends on very many."
- **Self-explanation prompt:** "In your own words, why does the sweep run from the output back to the inputs?"
- **Tutor.** Focus: the rule as a description of what the learner did. Avoid: "Jacobian" (lesson 21).
