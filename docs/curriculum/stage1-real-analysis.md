### 0.3 Conventions for every lesson

**Ids** follow `CONTENT-FORMAT.md` 4.1. Topic `top.real-analysis`; library namespace `ra` (`skl.ra.*`, `mc.ra.*`, `gen.ra.*`, `drl.ra.*`, `itm.ra.*`), which no sibling uses. Courses `crs.real-line` (RL) and `crs.real-functions` (FN); levels `lvl.<course>.<slug>`, lessons `lsn.<course>.<slug>`, homework `set.<course>.<level-slug>`, practice sets `set.<course>.practice`, topic exam `exm.analysis`, exit part `a1.exit`. The `#` column is the lesson's number within A1 (1 to 48); display numbers are computed from position and never stored.

**Every lesson** runs the design research 8.3 sequence: a hook on a canvas, free manipulation, the name for what the learner just did, a ladder of 2 to 4 screens adding one constraint each, the trap, the rule stated as a description of the learner's own steps with at least one derived line the learner completes, a transfer screen where a true quant or ML context exists, the summary card, then a quiz of 3 to 5 items. From the owner's skills, every lesson also carries the "why this exists" frame, one common trap, at least one edge case, a worked example with a decision point where the naive route fails, and a motivated leap wherever a creative step appears (`derive` screens carry `leap: naive, fails, pointsTo`). In analysis these take particular forms:

- **Manipulation before notation** means dragging an upper-bound bar until it stops being one, playing the adversary's $\varepsilon$ against your own $N$, stepping a bisection with the rationals-only switch on, probing a point's neighbourhood, or sliding $n$ while an $\varepsilon$-tube follows a curve, before any definition appears.
- **The worked example's decision point** is usually a choice the naive proof gets wrong: which quantity $N$ may depend on; whether to bound a factor before choosing $\delta$; whether a test's silence means anything; whether one sequence, or every sequence, must behave.
- **Derive** means completing the missing line of a proof (the $\frac\varepsilon2$ split, the bound on a convergent sequence, the auxiliary function), never only an algebraic step.
- **Quantifiers on screen.** Every definition is shown with its quantifiers as tiles in order, and every proof screen names what has been chosen and by whom (the adversary's $\varepsilon$, your $N$) before any inequality [our choice]: quantifier order is where analysis is lost, and naming the moves keeps it visible.

**Widgets** use the ids of `docs/WIDGETS.md`: the calculus composites (`epsilon-delta`, `limit-explorer`, `series-explorer`, `local-zoom`, `derivative-tracer`, `secant-tangent`, `taylor-builder`, `convexity-chord`, `accumulator`), the Stage 0 composites (`sequence-lab`, `iteration-cobweb`, `induction-chain`, `card-sort`), the probability composite `dist-explorer`, the primitives (`number-line`, `plot-2d`, `marks`, `drag-point`, `slider`, `stepper`, `value-table`, `live-formula`, `cell-grid`, `node-link`, `token-tray`) and the inputs (`choice`, `numeric-input`, `expression-input`, `derivation-steps`, `free-response`, and `code-cell` in homework only). Six new widgets carry ideas no existing widget shows: `bound-lab`, `quantifier-game`, `cardinality-lab`, `proof-builder`, `line-topology` and `uniform-lab` (section 8). A mode a widget does not yet list is marked [mode to add]; section 8.2 collects them.

**Bertie** follows decision 16. In widget screens he reads the widget's `describe()` and, in lesson and explore modes only, its `secret()` block (the exact supremum, the smallest $N$, the adversary's winning move) under the never-reveal instruction. He may offer a "Try this" chip that sets a slider or plays a move, which the learner taps.

**Quiz item types** (`CONTENT-FORMAT.md` 3.4), the letters of the siblings: N numeric (exact fractions and integers), X expression, C single choice, M multi-select, O order the steps, S sort or match, I interval or solution set. P, R, A, K and T are not used in A1 quizzes. Long-form items appear in homework and exams, never in quizzes. **Production items** (`ASSESSMENT.md` 2.3): the 37 lessons whose objective begins Prove each add one short production item, a 2 to 4 line proof in `free-response` marked against a 3-point rubric, as a fifth item, and their O items become "write the missing step" items; the counts in section 0.4 exclude the 37 production items. Single and multiple choice stay at or below 40% of each course's quiz items (design research 10).

**Skills.** Each level lists its skills at level grain (`ASSESSMENT.md` 4.1): 5 to 7 a level, at most 2 introduced by any lesson; finer distinctions (the $\frac\varepsilon2$ split, the first restriction $\delta \le \frac c2$) are step tags. Skill ids are `skl.ra.<verb-phrase>`.

**Misconceptions.** Every `mc.ra.*` id in the tables is new and joins the register. Where an A1 trap repeats a 1.3 error (the order of terms in an infinite sum, the remainder bounded at the centre), its author uses 1.3's id; the tables name only the new errors each trap screen is built on.

**Level review.** Every level ends with a Level review of 8 items at difficulty 2 to 4, interleaved, with up to 30% drawn from earlier levels of the same course (`ASSESSMENT.md` 2.4). The homework set follows (section 3).

**Columns.** Objective is one measurable sentence. Hook is the puzzle the lesson opens with, with its answer in brackets. Trap is the misconception the trap screen is built around; Edge is the fringe case the lesson must show. Quiz lists the item types in order, without the production item.

**Notation** (design research 9.4, Abbott's where he has one): $\mathbb N = \{1, 2, 3, \dots\}$; $\sup$ and $\inf$, with $\max$ and $\min$ only when attained; $(a_n)$ for a sequence and $(a_{n_k})$ for a subsequence; $\limsup$ and $\liminf$ for the limits of the tail suprema and infima; $V_\varepsilon(a) = (a - \varepsilon, a + \varepsilon)$ for an $\varepsilon$-neighbourhood; $\overline{A}$ for the closure; $f_n \to f$ for pointwise and "$f_n \to f$ uniformly" in words, never an arrow with a letter over it; $d_n = \sup_{x \in A}\lvert f_n(x) - f(x)\rvert$ for the sup gap (our name, used throughout); $P(A)$ for the power set; quantifiers $\forall$ and $\exists$ written in words in prose and as symbols only in definitions on screen.

## 1. Item overview

### 1.1 Purpose in a quant and ML-lab career

- **Research interviews ask for proofs, and ML theory is written in them.** "Show this estimator is consistent", "prove the iteration converges", "why does the limit of these models exist" are $\varepsilon$-$N$, compactness and uniform-convergence arguments. A researcher who cannot write them can use a result but cannot check whether a paper's lemma holds, which is the daily work of reading theory (3.1) and the content of researcher interview rounds (3.3 level TR3).
- **Probability on solid ground.** Countable additivity, the countably many atoms of a distribution, the Cantor distribution with no density, swapping a sum or a limit with an expectation: each is a statement of A1 before it is one of 1.5. A1 comes first on the two tracks so that 1.5's rules arrive as consequences, not as conventions.
- **Optimisation has to have an answer.** A continuous objective on a compact set attains its minimum (lesson 30); on an open set it may not, which is why a portfolio problem with weights confined to an open region can have no best portfolio (lesson 24's transfer). Convex functions have one-sided derivatives everywhere and corners only countably often (lesson 37): the subgradients that 2.0b and the lasso use.
- **Approximation is a theorem with hypotheses.** Uniform convergence decides whether a limit of continuous models is continuous, whether integrals and derivatives pass to the limit, and whether a training error can be trusted for the model the data chose (lesson 39). The Weierstrass approximation theorem (lesson 48) is the template of the universal approximation theorem for networks, and says just as little about how many units are needed.
- **Numerical honesty.** Completeness and the Cauchy criterion are why bisection and fixed-point solvers converge to a number at all, and why their stopping rules ("successive iterates agree") are safe only for contractions; floating point is a finite set of rationals with gaps (0.1 lesson 42, 2.0a).
- **The habit of hypotheses.** Every theorem in A1 fails when one hypothesis is dropped, and each lesson shows the counterexample. "Which hypothesis does this need?" is the question that catches a wrong result in a paper, a backtest or a model card.

### Course RL: The Real Line: Completeness, Sequences and Sets (`crs.real-line`, 5 levels, 26 lessons)

What makes the real numbers different from the rationals, and everything that difference buys. The rationals have the same arithmetic and order as the reals and a hole where $\sqrt2$ should be; one axiom, completeness, fills every such hole, and from it come the Archimedean property, nested intervals, the monotone convergence theorem, Bolzano-Weierstrass and the Cauchy criterion. On the way the course counts infinite sets (the rationals can be listed, the reals cannot), proves the series tests 1.3 used, shows when the order of an infinite sum matters, and ends with the topology of the line: open, closed, compact and connected sets, the Cantor set and Baire's theorem. Spine: Abbott chapters 1 to 3; MIT 18.100A lectures 1 to 12, pull-based [check numbers].

### Course FN: Functions: Continuity, Derivatives and Uniform Limits (`crs.real-functions`, 4 levels, 22 lessons)

What a function does near a point, across a compact set and in the limit of a sequence of functions. The course turns function limits into sequence limits, meets the functions that break intuition (Dirichlet's, Thomae's, $\lvert x \rvert^a\sin\frac1x$, a continuous function with no derivative anywhere), proves the extreme and intermediate value theorems that 1.3 used, separates continuity from uniform continuity, and asks the question that runs through modern analysis and machine learning: when does a limit of functions keep the properties of the functions? Uniform convergence answers it for continuity, integrals and derivatives, and power series, Taylor series and the Weierstrass approximation theorem are its payoff. Spine: Abbott chapters 4 to 6; MIT 18.100A lectures 13 to 25, pull-based [check numbers].

- *Example A (long-form, d4).* Let $A$ and $B$ be non-empty sets of reals, bounded above, and $A + B = \{a + b : a \in A,\ b \in B\}$. Prove that $\sup(A + B) = \sup A + \sup B$.
  *Model answer.* (1) Let $s = \sup A$ and $t = \sup B$, which exist by the Axiom of Completeness. (2) For $a \in A$ and $b \in B$, $a \le s$ and $b \le t$, so $a + b \le s + t$: $A + B$ is non-empty and bounded above by $s + t$, so $u = \sup(A + B)$ exists and $u \le s + t$. (3) For the reverse inequality, use lesson 4's characterisation: let $\varepsilon > 0$. (4) The leap: split the tolerance between the two sets. There is $a \in A$ with $a > s - \frac\varepsilon2$ and $b \in B$ with $b > t - \frac\varepsilon2$. (5) Then $a + b > s + t - \varepsilon$, and $a + b \le u$, so $u > s + t - \varepsilon$. (6) This holds for every $\varepsilon > 0$, so $u \ge s + t$ (lesson 2). (7) Hence $u = s + t$. (8) An accepted alternative to lines 3 to 6: fix $b$; every $a$ satisfies $a \le u - b$, so $s \le u - b$; then every $b$ satisfies $b \le u - s$, so $t \le u - s$.
  *Rubric (10).* 1: $s$ and $t$ exist (line 1). 2: $s + t$ is an upper bound and $u \le s + t$ (line 2). 3: the $\frac\varepsilon2$ choice of $a$ and $b$ (lines 3, 4), the creative step; line 8's route earns these 3 and the next 3. 2: $u > s + t - \varepsilon$ (line 5). 1: from every $\varepsilon$ to $u \ge s + t$ (line 6). 1: conclusion (line 7). Common losses: assuming $\sup A \in A$; choosing one $\varepsilon$ for both sets so the sum misses by $2\varepsilon$ without saying why that is enough.
- *Example B (d5).* Let $S = \{x \in \mathbb R : x^3 < 2\}$ and $\alpha = \sup S$. (a) Explain why $\alpha$ exists. (b) Prove that $\alpha^3 = 2$ by ruling out $\alpha^3 < 2$ and $\alpha^3 > 2$, each with an explicit $\frac1n$.
  *Answer.* (a) $0 \in S$, and if $x \ge 2$ then $x^3 \ge 8 > 2$, so 2 is an upper bound; the Axiom of Completeness gives $\alpha$, and $1 \in S$ gives $\alpha \ge 1$. (b) Suppose $\alpha^3 < 2$. For $0 < h \le 1$, $(\alpha + h)^3 = \alpha^3 + 3\alpha^2h + 3\alpha h^2 + h^3 \le \alpha^3 + h(3\alpha^2 + 3\alpha + 1)$, because $h^2 \le h$ and $h^3 \le h$. By the Archimedean property choose $n$ with $\frac1n < \frac{2 - \alpha^3}{3\alpha^2 + 3\alpha + 1}$; then $\left(\alpha + \frac1n\right)^3 < 2$, so $\alpha + \frac1n \in S$, contradicting that $\alpha$ is an upper bound. Suppose $\alpha^3 > 2$. For $0 < h \le \alpha$, $(\alpha - h)^3 = \alpha^3 - 3\alpha^2h + h^2(3\alpha - h) \ge \alpha^3 - 3\alpha^2h$. Choose $n$ with $\frac1n < \frac{\alpha^3 - 2}{3\alpha^2}$ and $\frac1n \le \alpha$; then $\left(\alpha - \frac1n\right)^3 > 2 > x^3$ for every $x \in S$, so every $x \in S$ is below $\alpha - \frac1n$ (cubing is increasing), a smaller upper bound, again a contradiction. So $\alpha^3 = 2$. Decision point: the naive route expands $\left(\alpha + \frac1n\right)^3$ and tries to choose $n$ from all four terms at once, which is a cubic inequality in $\frac1n$; bounding $h^2$ and $h^3$ by $h$ for $h \le 1$ turns it into a linear one that the Archimedean property solves.

- *Example A (long-form, d4).* Prove that $[0, 1]$ and $(0, 1)$ have the same cardinality by writing down a bijection.
  *Model answer.* (1) Let $T = \{0, 1\} \cup \left\{\frac1n : n \ge 2\right\}$, a countable subset of $[0, 1]$. (2) Define $f(0) = \frac12$, $f(1) = \frac13$, $f\left(\frac1n\right) = \frac{1}{n + 2}$ for $n \ge 2$, and $f(x) = x$ for $x \notin T$. (3) The leap is Hilbert's hotel: shift the list $0, 1, \frac12, \frac13, \dots$ two places along the list $\frac12, \frac13, \frac14, \dots$, which frees exactly the two places needed for 0 and 1. (4) Every value lies in $(0, 1)$: points of $T$ go to $\frac1k$ with $k \ge 2$, and other points are already in $(0, 1)$. (5) Injective: on $T$ the values $\frac12, \frac13, \frac14, \dots$ are distinct; off $T$, $f$ is the identity; and a value from $T$ lies in $\left\{\frac1k : k \ge 2\right\}$, which no value from outside $T$ does. (6) Surjective: take $y \in (0, 1)$. If $y = \frac12$, $y = f(0)$; if $y = \frac13$, $y = f(1)$; if $y = \frac1k$ with $k \ge 4$, $y = f\left(\frac{1}{k - 2}\right)$; otherwise $y \notin T$ and $y = f(y)$. (7) So $f$ is a bijection.
  *Rubric (10).* 2: the countable set and the map (lines 1, 2). 2: the shift idea stated (line 3), the creative step. 1: values in $(0, 1)$ (line 4). 2: injectivity, with the case across $T$ and its complement (line 5). 2: surjectivity, with all three cases (line 6). 1: conclusion (line 7). Accepted alternative: Schröder-Bernstein with the two injections $x \mapsto x$ and $x \mapsto \frac{x}{2} + \frac14$, if the learner states the theorem (not proved in A1) and checks both maps.
- *Example B (d5).* (a) Prove that the set of finite subsets of $\mathbb N$ is countable. (b) Prove that the set of all subsets of $\mathbb N$ is not.
  *Answer.* (a) Send a finite set $S$ to $\sigma(S) = \sum_{k \in S}2^{k-1}$, with $\sigma(\varnothing) = 0$. Every non-negative integer has exactly one binary expansion, so $\sigma$ is a bijection from the finite subsets onto $\{0, 1, 2, \dots\}$, which is countable. (b) By Cantor's theorem (lesson 9) no function from $\mathbb N$ onto $P(\mathbb N)$ exists, so $P(\mathbb N)$ is uncountable. Decision point: the naive list ("all sets of size 1, then all of size 2, ...") never finishes its first block, because there are infinitely many singletons; listing by binary code, or by largest element, puts every finite set at a finite position.

- *Example A (long-form, d4).* Prove that if $a_n \ge 0$ for every $n$ and $a_n \to a$, then $\sqrt{a_n} \to \sqrt a$.
  *Model answer.* (1) $a \ge 0$ by the order limit theorem (lesson 12), so $\sqrt a$ exists. (2) Case $a = 0$: let $\varepsilon > 0$ and choose $N$ with $a_n < \varepsilon^2$ for $n \ge N$; then $\sqrt{a_n} < \varepsilon$. (3) Case $a > 0$; the leap is the conjugate, which turns a difference of roots into a difference of the terms: $\sqrt{a_n} - \sqrt a = \frac{a_n - a}{\sqrt{a_n} + \sqrt a}$. (4) The denominator is at least $\sqrt a > 0$, so $\lvert\sqrt{a_n} - \sqrt a\rvert \le \frac{\lvert a_n - a \rvert}{\sqrt a}$. (5) Let $\varepsilon > 0$ and choose $N$ with $\lvert a_n - a \rvert < \varepsilon\sqrt a$ for $n \ge N$; then $\lvert\sqrt{a_n} - \sqrt a\rvert < \varepsilon$. (6) In both cases $\sqrt{a_n} \to \sqrt a$.
  *Rubric (10).* 1: $a \ge 0$ (line 1). 2: the case $a = 0$ with $\varepsilon^2$ (line 2). 3: the conjugate (line 3), the creative step. 2: the bound by $\frac{1}{\sqrt a}$ (line 4). 1: $N$ chosen from $\varepsilon\sqrt a$ (line 5). 1: conclusion covering both cases (line 6). - *Example B (d5).* $x_{n+1} = 4 - \frac{3}{x_n}$. (a) From $x_1 = 2$, prove that $(x_n)$ converges and find its limit. (b) Do the same from $x_1 = 5$. (c) From $x_1 = 1$ the sequence is constant at 1. Explain, with the derivative of $g(x) = 4 - \frac3x$, why starts near 1 other than 1 do not stay near it.
  *Answer.* The limit equation $L = 4 - \frac3L$ gives $L^2 - 4L + 3 = 0$, so $L \in \{1, 3\}$, and $x_{n+1} - x_n = -\frac{(x_n - 1)(x_n - 3)}{x_n}$. (a) Induction: if $1 < x_n < 3$, then $x_{n+1} - x_n > 0$, and $x_{n+1} < 3$ (because $\frac{3}{x_n} > 1$) and $x_{n+1} > 1$ (because $\frac{3}{x_n} < 3$). So the sequence ($2, \frac52, \frac{14}{5}, \frac{41}{14}, \frac{122}{41}, \dots$) increases and is bounded by 3; it converges by monotone convergence, its limit is at least 2, so it is 3. (b) If $x_n > 3$, then $x_{n+1} > 3$ and $x_{n+1} < x_n$; the sequence $5, \frac{17}{5}, \frac{53}{17}, \frac{161}{53}, \dots$ decreases, is bounded below by 3, and converges to 3. (c) $g'(x) = \frac{3}{x^2}$: $g'(3) = \frac13$, so near 3 each step shrinks the distance to 3 by about a third, while $g'(1) = 3$ triples the distance from 1. Decision point: the limit equation offers 1 and 3 and cannot choose; the induction bound chooses, and the derivative at each root explains which one attracts (1.3 lesson 86).

- *Example A (long-form, d4).* Prove that every Cauchy sequence of real numbers converges.
  *Model answer.* (1) Let $(a_n)$ be Cauchy. It is bounded: with $\varepsilon = 1$ choose $N$ with $\lvert a_m - a_n \rvert < 1$ for $m, n \ge N$; then $\lvert a_n \rvert < \lvert a_N \rvert + 1$ for $n \ge N$, so $\lvert a_n \rvert \le M = \max(\lvert a_1 \rvert, \dots, \lvert a_{N-1} \rvert, \lvert a_N \rvert + 1)$ for every $n$. (3) The leap: a Cauchy sequence with one convergent subsequence must follow it, because its terms are close to each other and some of them are close to $a$. (4) Let $\varepsilon > 0$ and choose $N$ with $\lvert a_m - a_n \rvert < \frac\varepsilon2$ for $m, n \ge N$. - *Example B (d5).* Let $a_{ij} = \frac{1}{i^2 - j^2}$ for $i \ne j$ and $a_{ii} = 0$, over $i, j \ge 1$. (a) For fixed $i$, prove that $\sum_j\frac{1}{j^2 - i^2}$ (over $j \ne i$) equals $\frac{3}{4i^2}$. (b) Deduce that summing rows first gives $-\frac{\pi^2}{8}$ and columns first gives $\frac{\pi^2}{8}$. (c) Say which hypothesis of lesson 21's theorem fails.
  *Answer.* (a) The series converges absolutely (its terms are of size $\frac{1}{j^2}$). Write $\frac{1}{j^2 - i^2} = \frac{1}{2i}\left(\frac{1}{j - i} - \frac{1}{j + i}\right)$ and sum to $J > 2i$, on partial sums: $\sum_{j \le J,\ j \ne i}\frac{1}{j - i} = H_{J-i} - H_{i-1}$ and $\sum_{j \le J,\ j \ne i}\frac{1}{j + i} = H_{J+i} - H_i - \frac{1}{2i}$, where $H_m$ is the $m$th harmonic number. (c) $\sum_{i,j}\lvert a_{ij} \rvert = \infty$: row $i$ alone contains $\lvert a_{i,i+1} \rvert = \frac{1}{2i + 1}$, and $\sum\frac{1}{2i + 1}$ diverges. - *Example A (long-form, d4).* Call $K \subseteq \mathbb R$ compact if every sequence in $K$ has a subsequence converging to a point of $K$. Prove that a compact set is closed and bounded.
  *Model answer.* (1) Let $K$ be compact. (2) Bounded, by contradiction: if not, for each $n$ pick $x_n \in K$ with $\lvert x_n \rvert > n$. (3) Every subsequence has $\lvert x_{n_k} \rvert > n_k \ge k$, so it is unbounded and cannot converge (convergent sequences are bounded, lesson 12): a contradiction. (4) Closed: let $x$ be a limit point of $K$. (5) The leap turns the limit point into a sequence: for each $n$ choose $x_n \in K$ with $0 < \lvert x_n - x \rvert < \frac1n$, so $x_n \to x$. (6) By compactness some subsequence converges to a point $y \in K$. (7) A subsequence of a convergent sequence has the same limit (lesson 16), so $y = x$ and $x \in K$. (8) $K$ contains its limit points, so it is closed (lesson 22).
  *Rubric (10).* 1: the definition (line 1). 3: boundedness by contradiction with the unbounded subsequence (lines 2, 3). 2: the sequence built from a limit point (line 5), the creative step. 2: the subsequence and uniqueness of limits (lines 6, 7). 2: closedness via the limit-point criterion (lines 4, 8).
- *Example B (d5).* Let $D$ be the set of $x \in [0, 1]$ with at least one decimal expansion that uses no digit 5. (a) Is $\frac12$ in $D$? (b) Prove that $D$ is compact. (c) Find its total length. (d) Is it countable?
  *Answer.* (a) Yes: $\frac12 = 0.4999\ldots$. (b) The points with first digit forced to be 5 form the open interval $(0.5, 0.6)$, and at stage $k$ the points whose $k$th digit is forced to be 5 form $9^{k-1}$ open intervals of length $10^{-k}$ inside what is left. $D$ is $[0, 1]$ with all of them removed, an intersection of finite unions of closed intervals, hence closed; it is bounded, so it is compact (lesson 24). (c) The removed length is $\sum_{k \ge 1}\frac{9^{k-1}}{10^k} = \frac{1/10}{1 - 9/10} = 1$, so $D$ has length 0. (d) It is uncountable: relabel the digits $0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 7, 8, 9$ as $0, \dots, 8$ and read the expansion in base 9; this maps $D$ onto $[0, 1]$. - *Example A (long-form, d4).* Prove that a function continuous on a compact set $K$ is uniformly continuous on $K$.
  *Model answer.* (1) Suppose not. Negating the definition (lesson 2): there is $\varepsilon_0 > 0$ such that for every $\delta > 0$ there are $x, y \in K$ with $\lvert x - y \rvert < \delta$ and $\lvert f(x) - f(y) \rvert \ge \varepsilon_0$. (2) Take $\delta = \frac1n$: this gives $x_n, y_n \in K$ with $\lvert x_n - y_n \rvert < \frac1n$ and $\lvert f(x_n) - f(y_n) \rvert \ge \varepsilon_0$. (3) By compactness a subsequence $x_{n_k}$ converges to some $x \in K$. (4) Then $y_{n_k} \to x$ as well, because $\lvert y_{n_k} - x \rvert \le \lvert y_{n_k} - x_{n_k} \rvert + \lvert x_{n_k} - x \rvert \to 0$. (5) The leap: continuity at the one point $x$ pulls both images to $f(x)$: $f(x_{n_k}) \to f(x)$ and $f(y_{n_k}) \to f(x)$ (the sequential criterion, lesson 27). (6) So $\lvert f(x_{n_k}) - f(y_{n_k}) \rvert \to 0$, contradicting line 2. (7) Hence $f$ is uniformly continuous.
  *Rubric (10).* 2: the negation with its quantifiers in order (line 1). 1: the two sequences (line 2). 2: compactness for $x_{n_k}$ (line 3). 1: $y_{n_k} \to x$ (line 4). 2: continuity at $x$ applied to both (line 5), the creative step. 1: the contradiction (line 6). 1: conclusion (line 7). Accepted alternative: an open-cover proof by Heine-Borel. Common losses: a second application of compactness to $(y_n)$, giving a limit that need not equal $x$ without line 4.
- *Example B (d5).* $f : \mathbb R \to \mathbb R$ satisfies $f(x + y) = f(x) + f(y)$ for all $x, y$ and is continuous at 0. Prove that $f(x) = cx$ for every $x$, where $c = f(1)$.
  *Answer.* $f(0) = f(0 + 0) = 2f(0)$, so $f(0) = 0$, and $f(-x) = -f(x)$. By induction $f(nx) = nf(x)$ for $n \in \mathbb N$, so $f(n) = nc$, and $nf\left(\frac mn\right) = f(m) = mc$ gives $f(q) = cq$ for every rational $q$. Continuity at 0 gives continuity everywhere: $f(x + h) - f(x) = f(h) \to f(0) = 0$ as $h \to 0$. For real $x$ choose rationals $q_n \to x$ (lesson 5); then $f(x) = \lim f(q_n) = \lim cq_n = cx$ by the sequential criterion. Decision point: the naive proof assumes $f$ is linear, or differentiable; additivity plus continuity at a single point is all that is needed, and without any continuity other solutions exist, built from a basis of $\mathbb R$ over $\mathbb Q$ (named only), whose graphs are dense in the plane.

- *Example A (long-form, d4).* Let $f$ be differentiable on $[a, b]$ with $f'(a) < \alpha < f'(b)$. Prove that $f'(c) = \alpha$ for some $c \in (a, b)$.
  *Model answer.* (1) Let $g(x) = f(x) - \alpha x$, differentiable on $[a, b]$ with $g'(a) = f'(a) - \alpha < 0 < g'(b) = f'(b) - \alpha$. (2) $g$ is continuous on the compact $[a, b]$, so it attains a minimum at some $c$ (lesson 30). (3) The leap: the signs of $g'$ at the ends push the minimum inside. Since $g'(a) < 0$, the quotient $\frac{g(x) - g(a)}{x - a}$ is negative for $x$ slightly above $a$, so $g(x) < g(a)$ there and $a$ is not the minimum. (4) Since $g'(b) > 0$, the quotient $\frac{g(x) - g(b)}{x - b}$ is positive for $x$ slightly below $b$, and $x - b < 0$, so $g(x) < g(b)$ and $b$ is not the minimum. (5) So $c \in (a, b)$ is an interior minimum of a differentiable function, and $g'(c) = 0$ (Fermat, 1.3 lesson 35). (6) Hence $f'(c) = \alpha$. (7) Continuity of $f'$ was never used.
  *Rubric (10).* 1: $g$ and its end derivatives (line 1). 2: the extreme value theorem (line 2). 3: both endpoint arguments (lines 3, 4), the creative step. 2: Fermat at an interior minimum (line 5). 1: conclusion (line 6). 1: the remark on continuity (line 7).
- *Example B (d5).* $f$ is differentiable on $[0, 1]$, $f(0) = 0$, and $\lvert f'(x) \rvert \le \lvert f(x) \rvert$ for every $x$. Prove that $f = 0$ on $[0, 1]$.
  *Answer.* Let $M = \max_{[0, 1/2]}\lvert f \rvert$, attained at some $x_0$ (lesson 30). If $x_0 = 0$, then $M = \lvert f(0) \rvert = 0$ at once. Otherwise, by the mean value theorem, $f(x_0) = f(x_0) - f(0) = f'(c)x_0$ for some $c \in (0, x_0)$, so $M = \lvert f(x_0) \rvert \le \lvert f(c) \rvert \cdot \frac12 \le \frac M2$, and $M = 0$. So $f = 0$ on $\left[0, \frac12\right]$; repeat on $\left[\frac12, 1\right]$ starting from $f\left(\frac12\right) = 0$. Decision point: the comparison argument for differential equations (Grönwall) is not on the path, P8 not being adopted; compactness plus the mean value theorem does it in three lines, and the half-interval is the creative step: on all of $[0, 1]$ the same estimate gives only $M \le M$.

- *Example A (long-form, d4).* (a) Let $f_n \to f$ uniformly on $A$, with every $f_n$ continuous at $c \in A$. Prove that $f$ is continuous at $c$. (b) Use (a) to show that $x^n$ does not converge uniformly on $[0, 1]$, and confirm it by computing the sup gap.
  *Model answer.* (1) Let $\varepsilon > 0$. (2) By uniform convergence choose $N$ with $\lvert f_N(x) - f(x) \rvert < \frac\varepsilon3$ for every $x \in A$, one $N$ for all $x$. (4) For such $x$: $\lvert f(x) - f(c) \rvert \le \lvert f(x) - f_N(x) \rvert + \lvert f_N(x) - f_N(c) \rvert + \lvert f_N(c) - f(c) \rvert < \varepsilon$. (5) So $f$ is continuous at $c$.   *Rubric (10).* 1: set-up (line 1). 2: $N$ independent of $x$ (line 2). 2: $\delta$ from $f_N$'s continuity (line 3). 2: the three-term split (line 4). 1: conclusion (line 5). 2: the contrapositive with where the proof breaks, and the sup gap (lines 6, 7).
- *Example B (d5).* On $[0, 1]$ let $g_n(x) = n^2xe^{-nx}$ and $f_n(x) = nxe^{-nx}$. (a) Find both pointwise limits. (b) Find both sup gaps. (c) Find $\int_0^1g_n$ and $\int_0^1f_n$ and their limits. (d) What do (b) and (c) say about lesson 41's theorem?

- *Example A (long-form, d4).* Let $f$ have $N + 1$ derivatives on $(-R, R)$ and let $S_N$ be its Taylor polynomial of degree $N$ at 0. (a) Prove that for each $x \ne 0$ in $(-R, R)$ there is $c$ strictly between 0 and $x$ with $f(x) - S_N(x) = \frac{f^{(N+1)}(c)}{(N + 1)!}x^{N+1}$. (b) Deduce that the Taylor series of $e^x$ converges to $e^x$ for every real $x$.
  *Model answer.* (1) Let $E(t) = f(t) - S_N(t)$. Because $S_N$ matches $f$ and its first $N$ derivatives at 0, $E(0) = E'(0) = \dots = E^{(N)}(0) = 0$, and $E^{(N+1)} = f^{(N+1)}$, since $S_N$ has degree $N$. (2) The leap: compare $E$ with $t^{N+1}$, which also vanishes to order $N$ at 0, using the generalised mean value theorem (lesson 36) once for each derivative. (3) On $[0, x]$ (or $[x, 0]$), for $E$ and $t^{N+1}$: $\frac{E(x)}{x^{N+1}} = \frac{E'(x_1)}{(N + 1)x_1^N}$ for some $x_1$ strictly between 0 and $x$, using $E(0) = 0$. (5) After $N + 1$ steps, $\frac{E(x)}{x^{N+1}} = \frac{E^{(N+1)}(c)}{(N + 1)!}$ with $c = x_{N+1}$ strictly between 0 and $x$. (6) So $E(x) = \frac{f^{(N+1)}(c)}{(N + 1)!}x^{N+1}$. (7) For $e^x$, $\lvert f^{(N+1)}(c) \rvert \le e^{\lvert x \rvert}$, and $\frac{\lvert x \rvert^{N+1}}{(N + 1)!} \to 0$ for every $x$ (the ratio of consecutive terms tends to 0), so $E(x) \to 0$ and the series converges to $e^x$.
  *Rubric (10).* 2: $E$ and its vanishing derivatives (line 1). 2: the comparison idea (line 2), the creative step. 2: the first generalised mean value step with its hypotheses (line 3). 2: the iteration to step $N + 1$ (lines 4, 5). 1: conclusion (line 6). 1: the application to $e^x$, with the derivative bound (line 7). ### 4.2 Two specimen proof slots in full

**Q13 (specimen, d3): an infimum identity.** "Let $A$ be a non-empty set of reals, bounded above, let $c < 0$, and let $cA = \{ca : a \in A\}$. Prove that $\inf(cA) = c\sup A$."

*Model answer.* (1) Let $s = \sup A$, which exists by the Axiom of Completeness. (2) For $a \in A$, $a \le s$, and multiplying by $c < 0$ reverses the inequality: $ca \ge cs$. So $cs$ is a lower bound of $cA$. (3) The leap: to show no larger number is a lower bound, use the supremum's $\varepsilon$ characterisation with the tolerance rescaled by $\lvert c \rvert$. Let $\varepsilon > 0$, and choose $a \in A$ with $a > s - \frac{\varepsilon}{\lvert c \rvert}$. (4) Multiplying by $c < 0$: $ca < cs - \frac{c\varepsilon}{\lvert c \rvert} = cs + \varepsilon$. (5) So $cs + \varepsilon$ is not a lower bound of $cA$ for any $\varepsilon > 0$, and $cs$ is the greatest lower bound: $\inf(cA) = c\sup A$.

*Rubric (10).* 1: $s$ exists (line 1). 2: $cs$ is a lower bound, with the reversal (line 2). 3: the choice of $a$ with the rescaled tolerance (line 3), the creative step. 2: the inequality with the sign handled (line 4). 2: the infimum's characterisation completed (line 5). Common losses: the inequality not reversed; $\varepsilon$ used where $\frac{\varepsilon}{\lvert c \rvert}$ is needed, then a claim of $cs + \lvert c \rvert\varepsilon$ without saying that it is enough.

**Q16 (specimen, d5): a series of functions.** "Let $f(x) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{\sin(nx)}{n^3}$. (a) Prove that $f$ is continuous on $\mathbb R$. (b) Prove that $f$ is differentiable with $f'(x) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{\cos(nx)}{n^2}$. (c) Explain why the same route does not give $f''$. (d) You may use that $\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{\cos(nx)}{n^2} = \frac{\pi^2}{6} - \frac{\pi x}{2} + \frac{x^2}{4}$ for $0 \le x \le 2\pi$. Show that $f'$ has no derivative at 0."

*Model answer.* (1) Each term is continuous and $\left\lvert\frac{\sin(nx)}{n^3}\right\rvert \le \frac{1}{n^3}$, with $\sum\frac{1}{n^3}$ convergent, so the series converges uniformly on $\mathbb R$ by the M-test (lesson 43) and $f$ is continuous (lesson 40). (2) The derived series $\sum\frac{\cos(nx)}{n^2}$ has terms bounded by $\frac{1}{n^2}$, so it converges uniformly on $\mathbb R$, again by the M-test. (3) The original series converges at a point (everywhere, by line 1), so by lesson 42 $f$ is differentiable and $f'$ is the sum of the derived series. (4) The second derived series is $-\sum\frac{\sin(nx)}{n}$; its terms are bounded only by $\frac1n$, and $\sum\frac1n$ diverges, so the M-test gives nothing. A failed M-test proves nothing either way; it only removes the route. (5) The leap of part (d): the given formula describes $f'$ to the right of 0, and $f'$ is even ($\cos$ is even), so it describes $f'(-x)$ too. (6) For small $x > 0$, $\frac{f'(x) - f'(0)}{x} = -\frac{\pi}{2} + \frac{x}{4} \to -\frac\pi2$; by evenness, the quotient from the left tends to $+\frac\pi2$. (7) The one-sided limits differ, so $f''(0)$ does not exist: the corner of lesson 37, at the level of the derivative.

*Rubric (10).* 2: continuity by the M-test and lesson 40 (line 1). 3: differentiability by lesson 42, with both hypotheses checked (lines 2, 3). 2: why the route fails, without claiming the failure proves non-uniformity (line 4). 3: the one-sided derivatives of $f'$ at 0 from the formula and evenness (lines 5 to 7), the creative step. Common losses: concluding from the failed M-test that the second series diverges or is not uniform; differentiating the formula without noting it holds only on $[0, 2\pi]$ [the formula checked with mpmath at four points].

## 6. Verification approach

Every graded answer is computed twice, by two implementations that share no code (`CONTENT-FORMAT.md` 6.2): the item's generator or widget model in TypeScript (`@zq/models/analysis`), and the Python verifier `zqverify` run in CI on CPython 3.12 with the pinned stack, written from the generator's plain-language `semantics`. Disagreement fails the build. Analysis adds four rules of its own [our choice]:

1. **Exact or not at all.** Every rational answer is computed in exact rational arithmetic on both sides (`Fraction` in Python, a big-rational type in `@zq/models`); every closed form is checked by SymPy; floating point appears only as a cross-check, never as the key.
2. **Smallest means proved smallest.** An answer of the form "the smallest $N$" or "the first degree" is checked twice: the gap or bound is shown monotone in $n$ symbolically (SymPy, on the generator's family), and the boundary values $N - 1$ and $N$ are evaluated exactly. A family whose gap is not monotone is rejected by the generator.
3. **Every counterexample is run.** Each counterexample a lesson, item or model answer states (the double sum's $\pm\frac{\pi^2}{8}$, $x^n$'s sup gap of 1, the bump whose integrals tend to 1, Riemann's rearrangement reaching 2) is computed in CI, to 30 digits where it is a series value.
4. **A proof is checked by people; its arithmetic by machines.** CI cannot decide whether a proof is correct. It checks every equation and inequality line of a model answer with SymPy (each follows from the last, or is flagged for the reviewer), that every rubric point names lines that exist, and that every "accepted alternative" in a rubric has its own model lines. The paid reviewer of owner decision 10 reads every model proof of a level before the level ships, and checks that each rubric admits the standard alternative proofs.

## 7. Sample lesson in full: "Pointwise against uniform convergence"

**Why this lesson** (decision 14). It is the item's central idea in one lesson: the order of two quantifiers decides whether a limit of continuous functions is continuous, whether integrals and derivatives pass to the limit (lessons 40 to 42) and whether a power series behaves (lessons 44 to 46). It reuses what the learner already owns: $N(\varepsilon)$ from lesson 11, the two quantifier orders of uniform continuity from lesson 31, and suprema from lesson 3. Its hook is a surprise anyone can see (continuous curves whose limit jumps), its trap is the one almost every learner falls into, and its transfer is the most important one in A1 for ML research: a training error can be trusted only if the gap between training and true error is small for every model at once.

**Screen 10. The rule, named** (`generalise`, id `named`)

- **Rule (prose):** "$f_n \to f$ **pointwise** on $A$ if for every $x \in A$ and every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is an $N$, which may depend on $x$ and $\varepsilon$, with $\lvert f_n(x) - f(x) \rvert < \varepsilon$ for all $n \ge N$. The convergence is **uniform** if $N$ can be chosen from $\varepsilon$ alone, for every $x$ at once. Equivalently, the sup gap $d_n = \sup_{x \in A}\lvert f_n(x) - f(x) \rvert$ tends to 0. You test it by computing the pointwise limit, then the worst gap for each $n$."
- **Tex:** pointwise: $\forall\varepsilon > 0\ \forall x \in A\ \exists N\ \forall n \ge N : \lvert f_n(x) - f(x) \rvert < \varepsilon$; uniform: $\forall\varepsilon > 0\ \exists N\ \forall x \in A\ \forall n \ge N : \lvert f_n(x) - f(x) \rvert < \varepsilon$.
- **Widget:** `quantifier-game` with the four tiles $\forall\varepsilon$, $\exists N$, $\forall x$, $\forall n \ge N$ shown in each option's order.
- **Confirmation question:** "Which statement says $f_n \to f$ uniformly on $A$?" (`choice`)
- **Correct:** A.

| Option | Correct? | Misconception | Feedback |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. $\forall\varepsilon > 0\ \exists N\ \forall x \in A\ \forall n \ge N$ | yes | | "Yes: $N$ is chosen before $x$, so it cannot depend on it." |
| B. $\forall\varepsilon > 0\ \forall x \in A\ \exists N\ \forall n \ge N$ | no | `mc.ra.quantifier-order-irrelevant` | "That is pointwise: $x$ comes first, so $N$ may depend on it." |
| C. $\exists N\ \forall\varepsilon > 0\ \forall x \in A\ \forall n \ge N$ | no | `mc.ra.quantifier-order-irrelevant` | "With $N$ chosen before $\varepsilon$, the gap must be below every $\varepsilon$ from $N$ on, so $f_n = f$ exactly for $n \ge N$: far too strong." |
| D. $\forall x \in A\ \exists N\ \forall\varepsilon > 0\ \forall n \ge N$ | no | `mc.ra.quantifier-order-irrelevant` | "That forces $f_n(x) = f(x)$ exactly from some $N$ on, at each $x$." |

- **Why?** Shown: "Swapping $\exists N$ and $\forall x$ is the whole difference, the same swap that separates continuity from uniform continuity (lesson 31)."
- **Self-explanation prompt:** "In your own words: what does it mean that $N$ comes before $x$?"
- **Tutor.** Focus: the rule as a description of what the learner did on screens 4 to 8. Avoid: "equicontinuous", "Arzelà-Ascoli".

**Screen 11. Transfer: trusting a training error** (`problem`, id `generalisation`)

- **Prose:** "A team fits a model by choosing, from a family of candidate settings $\theta$, the one with the lowest error on the training data. For any one $\theta$ fixed in advance, its training error $\hat R_n(\theta)$ converges to its true error $R(\theta)$ as the data grow: the law of large numbers, which 1.5 will prove, pointwise in $\theta$. But the chosen $\hat\theta$ depends on the data."
- **Widget:** `uniform-lab` in family mode: a fixture of 40 candidate settings, each with its true error and its training error at a chosen sample size, drawn as two rows of dots, the selected setting circled; a sample-size slider. The fixture is computed in CI on a stored seed; nothing is graded from it.
- **Question:** "Which quantity must be small for the chosen model's training error to be trusted?"
- **Answer input:** `choice`.
- **Correct:** B.

| Option | Correct? | Misconception | Feedback |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. $\lvert\hat R_n(\theta) - R(\theta)\rvert$ for one $\theta$ chosen before seeing the data | no | `mc.ra.fixed-model-suffices` | "That protects a model fixed in advance. The team chose $\hat\theta$ after looking, and it was chosen because its training error looked low." |
| B. $\sup_\theta\lvert\hat R_n(\theta) - R(\theta)\rvert$, the gap for every candidate at once | yes | | "Yes: if the worst gap is small, the gap at whatever $\hat\theta$ the data pick is small too." |
| C. The training error at $\hat\theta$ itself | no | (selection) | "That is the number in doubt, not a check on it." |
| D. The average gap over the candidates | no | `mc.ra.sample-points-are-worst` (in reverse) | "An average can be small while the gap at the chosen model, picked for looking good, is large." |

- **Hints.** Nudge: "The data choose $\hat\theta$. Which $\theta$ must the guarantee cover?" Point: highlights the circled dot. Step: "Since $\hat\theta$ could be any candidate, the gap must be small at all of them at once: a sup over $\theta$."
- **Why?** Yours: "$\sup_\theta\lvert\hat R_n(\theta) - R(\theta)\rvert$." Then: "That is uniform convergence of the training error over the model class, the basis of generalisation bounds (Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David, chapter 4). It is also why the best of a hundred backtested strategies looks better than it is: its in-sample edge is a maximum over many noisy gaps (3.1)."
- **Tutor.** Focus: why the selected model needs a uniform statement. Notes: the learner need not know any learning theory; the point is the order of "choose $\theta$" and "make the gap small". Reps: picture. Avoid: VC dimension and other machinery; "overfitting" as a slogan without the sup.

**Screen 12. Summary** (`summary`)

- **Ideas:**
  1. "Pointwise convergence lets $N$ depend on the point; uniform convergence asks for one $N$ for every point at once."
  2. "Test uniformity with the sup gap $d_n = \sup_x\lvert f_n(x) - f(x) \rvert$: compute the pointwise limit, find the worst point for each $n$ (often by calculus), and ask whether $d_n \to 0$."
  3. "The set matters: $x^n$ converges uniformly on $\left[0, \frac12\right]$ and not on $[0, 1]$, where continuous functions converge to a discontinuous limit."
- **Trap:** "Every point converges, so all of them do together: false, because the $N$ each point needs can grow without bound."
- **Card** (saved to the Notebook): pointwise $\forall\varepsilon\ \forall x\ \exists N$; uniform $\forall\varepsilon\ \exists N\ \forall x$; uniform exactly when $d_n = \sup_x\lvert f_n - f \rvert \to 0$.
