# Level briefs: mm-games (Market-Making Games, topic 3.3)

Course context. `mm-games` trains the trader-interview games of topic 3.3, Interview preparation (track `speed`; `docs/curriculum.md` section 3.3: "Market-making games: quote a two-sided price on an unknown quantity and update it as trades come in. Practise aloud with a partner. Betting games: expected value, when to hedge, Kelly sizing", plus Jane Street's free Probability and Markets guide, "written by the firm about its own interviews", and mock interviews spoken aloud because interviewers mark the reasoning as it happens; the curriculum's Stage 2 track table says trader interviews test "probability, mental arithmetic, market-making and betting games"). Name the Jane Street guide once per level, with Zhou's green book and Crack's Heard on the Street for the estimation and betting questions, and never quote their text. The games this course must cover, mapped to its first 13 lessons (level 4 adds five formats trading firms report: 90% intervals, arbitrage in a set of quotes, several contracts on one outcome, options on a game and a betting board): making a market on an unknown quantity (l1, l4, l5: bid, ask, width and the fair value inside them); the dice and coin-flip betting games priced by expectation (l2, l6); width from uncertainty (l3); updating on trades (l7: being lifted or hit is information, and inventory skew, linking `micro` l10's rule m = V − γq); adverse selection in a game where the counterparty knows more (l8, linking `micro` l11's s = αδ); updating as information is revealed (l9); inventory under a position limit, the Kelly bet-sizing game and the expected value against variance trade-off under a bankroll (l10); hedging with a correlated instrument (l11); thinking aloud (l12); and a mock round (l13, and every level's homework), graded by numeric checks on the quotes in lessons and by a `longform` rubric in homework (the validator allows `longform` only in homework and exams, so no lesson may use it). The learner has `micro` (l1 bids and asks, l5 the cost of crossing the spread, l7 where is the price, l9 quoting both sides, l10 skewing for inventory with V = 500p, s = 2p, γ = 1p per lot, l11 adverse selection with α = 0.2 and δ = 10p giving s = 2p), `conditional` (Bayes, l9 natural frequencies), `random-vars` (l6 a fair price for a bet, l9 measuring spread), `markov` (l10 gambler's ruin), `fin-ml` l9 (the Kelly fraction `p − (1 − p)/b`), `mental` (Stage 0 speed), and, in the same topic, `puzzles` (l21 derives Kelly; this course plays it) and `mental-pressure`. Use £ for money throughout and pence only where the `micro` numbers are reused. A game is played in the app as a scripted sequence of screens: the "interviewer" is the prose, the learner's quote is a `table` widget or `sliders` (bid and ask) graded by tolerance against a band of acceptable quotes (for example a bid between fair − 2 sd and fair, an ask between fair and fair + 2 sd, a width no wider than a stated limit) via `sliders` `check` expressions, and each trade the interviewer makes is the next screen; there is no live opponent, no clock (lesson screens have none; `docs/ENGINE-BACKLOG.md` 129 is deferred), and every graded number is exact or has a stated tolerance. Every number below was computed by code when the brief was written; those scripts are not kept, so recompute every number you use in your own scratchpad subfolder before stating it.

Interview form (levels 2 to 4; level 1 is already written to its own notes). Every game screen keeps a running ledger (trade, side, price, size, position, P&L at the fair value) in a `table` canvas, and at least one drill per lesson asks for the P&L, because interviewers ask for it after every trade. The trading-game widget (E12 in `docs/briefs/interview-plan.md`: a `trade` widget over the `orderbook` canvas with a seeded counterparty) is not built. Until it is, every game is a seeded script of counterparty actions, one per turn (fix it with `random.Random(seed)` in your scratchpad and list it in your report), each turn a screen with the fixed trade, written so that it can move to E12 without changing its numbers. Spoken answers are `longform` items in homework, because lessons may not hold `longform`: the learner answers aloud, then writes the answer and ticks the 10-point rubric. There is no audio recording (a user decision), and a preparation or answer countdown waits for the timed-set engine item (E6, not built), so until then the prose states the time and the learner keeps it. Firm names stay out of screens ("trading firms"; firm facts live in `recruiting` l4).

Shared runtime facts: Python runs in Pyodide 0.27.7 (NumPy and the rest exist, but the course's code screens should be pure Python with `random.Random(seed)` and `fractions`, so nothing slow loads); each test has about 5 s; no network. Canvases the course leans on: `orderbook` (bids, asks, `spreadValue: false` for the learner to work out, `order` to walk a trade), `number-line` (fair value as a point, bid and ask as draggable points or an interval, `derive` captions for the width), `bars` with `exact` for the distribution of a dice sum or a card sum, `distribution` (normal and discrete families with `shade` for the probability a quote is through), `series` for a P&L path with `drawdown: "money"`, `tree` for information and adverse selection, `table` for trade blotters, `plane` for P&L against width.
