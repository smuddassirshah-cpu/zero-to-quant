# Level briefs: nn-train (Training Networks, topic 2.7)

Course header. `nn-train` is the first course of topic 2.7, Deep learning, strand `ai` ("Not needed for the quant path. Machine learning research roles, and reading deep learning papers."). It has 4 levels and 16 lessons, skills `nt-optim`, `nt-signal`, `nt-general` and `nt-experiment`, one per level. - l1 (steps from a minibatch): hook: at one parameter value of the spiral net, the learner sees 200 minibatch gradients of the first weight for batch size 8 and for 128 on a `distribution`, and predicts how the spread changes. Named: a minibatch gradient is an unbiased estimate of the full gradient when the batch is drawn uniformly; its standard deviation falls like $1/\sqrt B$, so batch 128 has half the spread of batch 32 ($\sqrt{128/32} = 2$; the hypothesis is independent draws). Ladder: one SGD step written by hand on a three-point batch; the loss curve of full-batch GD against SGD at batch 32 (SGD is noisy but cheaper per step; compare at equal numbers of examples seen); a fixed step settles into a noisy band whose width grows with the step size (a `series` with a slider); choosing the step from the `makemore` L2 sweep (cite it, one screen, no reteach). Trap: "a smaller batch is always worse": per example seen it often wins early. Edge case: batch size equal to the data set (plain GD, no noise) and batch size 1. Generalise: the step size and the batch size set the noise together (one sentence on the linear scaling rule as a heuristic, with its conditions). `code` screens: `minibatch_indices(n, B, seed)` returning a list of index arrays covering one epoch without repeats (exact on seed), `sgd_step(params, grads, lr)` (exact), `grad_spread(B, trials, seed)` returning the sd of one coordinate's minibatch gradient on a supplied linear-regression loss, compared against the $1/\sqrt B$ prediction with a `tol`. Transfer: a desk fitting a return model on a million rows cannot take full-batch steps; what batch size and step to try first, and why the loss curve looks noisy.
- l2 (momentum): hook: on the convex l14 bowl the learner runs GD at its best fixed step $2/11$ and sees the rate $9/11 \approx 0.818$ per step, then turns on momentum with a slider on $\beta$ and watches the zigzag damp. Named: heavy-ball momentum $v_{k+1} = \beta v_k - s\nabla f(x_k)$, $x_{k+1} = x_k + v_{k+1}$; under a constant gradient the velocity settles at $s/(1 - \beta)$ times the gradient, so $\beta = 0.9$ is a tenfold step ($1/(1 - 0.9) = 10$; 0.99 gives 100). Ladder: two momentum steps by hand on the bowl from $(10, 1)$; the best heavy-ball settings on a quadratic with condition number $\kappa = 10$: $\beta = \left(\frac{\sqrt\kappa - 1}{\sqrt\kappa + 1}\right)^2 \approx 0.270$ and $s = \frac{4}{(\sqrt{\lambda_{\max}} + \sqrt{\lambda_{\min}})^2} \approx 0.231$, giving an asymptotic rate $\sqrt\beta \approx 0.519$ per step against GD's 0.818; after 20 steps that is about $2.0 \times 10^{-6}$ against 0.018 (asymptotic factors; the number script also measures the actual iterates, which carry an extra polynomial factor because the iteration matrix has a repeated eigenvalue); momentum on the spiral net against plain SGD (`series`). Trap: "momentum always helps": with $\beta$ near 1 and the same step the iterates overshoot and oscillate (the learner finds a $\beta$ that diverges on the bowl). Edge case: on a perfectly round bowl ($\kappa = 1$) the best $\beta$ is 0. Derive: the velocity under a constant gradient as a geometric series, an `order` item with a justification per line. `code` screens: `momentum_step(x, v, grad, s, beta)` returning the new pair (exact), `run_momentum(grad, x0, s, beta, n)` returning all iterates (tested on the bowl against a closed-form iterate count), `best_heavy_ball(lmin, lmax)` returning $(s, \beta)$ (exact to 1e-9). Transfer: fitting a factor model whose features are highly correlated gives a long narrow valley (the `convex` l14 ridge example); momentum is the cheap fix before a second-order method.
- l3 (Adam): hook: multiply the spiral net's loss by 1,000 and run SGD and Adam with unchanged settings; SGD diverges, Adam's path barely changes. The learner predicts which before running. Named: Adam keeps a moving average of the gradient $m$ and of its square $v$, divides one by the root of the other, and corrects both for starting at zero. With $\beta_1 = 0.9$ and $\beta_2 = 0.999$ the correction factors at step 1 are $1 - 0.9 = 0.1$ and $1 - 0.999 = 0.001$, so the first step is $\text{lr} \cdot g/|g|$ per coordinate, a step of exactly lr in size whatever the gradient's scale ($\epsilon$ aside); at step 10 they are $1 - 0.9^{10} \approx 0.651$ and $1 - 0.999^{10} \approx 0.00996$. Ladder: three Adam steps by hand on a one-parameter loss; without bias correction a constant gradient's step is scaled by $(1 - \beta_1^t)/\sqrt{1 - \beta_2^t}$, which is $0.1/\sqrt{0.001} \approx 3.16$ at step 1, about 6.53 at step 10, 3.24 at step 100 and 1.26 at step 1,000, so the early steps are several times too large, not too small (the learner computes step 1 and step 10; the trap distractor says "too small"); Adam against momentum on the spiral net, with the final losses in `bars`. Trap: "Adam needs no tuning": its learning rate still matters, and a rate that suits SGD is far too large for Adam (a sweep shows it). Edge case: a coordinate whose gradient is always 0 gets $v = 0$, and $\epsilon$ is what stops a division by zero. Generalise: Adam is a per-coordinate step size; RMSProp is Adam without $m$ (one sentence); AdamW is level 3's business (forward pointer only). `code` screens: `adam_step(params, grads, m, v, t, lr, b1, b2, eps)` returning updated params and moments (exact on supplied arrays), `adam_first_step_size(g, lr)` (exact), `train_spiral(opt, steps, seed)` returning the final training loss for `"sgd"`, `"momentum"` and `"adam"` with tuned settings, tested by threshold and by order (Adam and momentum below SGD at equal steps on this seed; the number script checks three seeds). - l4 (learning-rate schedules): hook: a `series` of the spiral net's loss under a constant rate, a step decay and a cosine decay; the learner predicts which ends lowest before seeing the last 50 steps. Named: a schedule changes the rate during training; linear warm-up over the first $w$ steps, then cosine decay $\text{lr}_t = \text{lr}_{\max} \cdot \tfrac12\left(1 + \cos(\pi t/T)\right)$, which is half of $\text{lr}_{\max}$ halfway through.
