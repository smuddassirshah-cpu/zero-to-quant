## Level 1

Author `ai-papers` level 1 ("How to read an ML paper"): `l1-the-claim`, `l2-baselines`, `l3-ablations`, `l4-plots-on-log-axes`, `review-1.json`, `homework-1.json`. Skills: `ap-read` on all four. Report to `docs/reports/ai-papers-1.md`.

- **Context.** `momentum-rep` L1 taught reading a finance paper in a fixed order. An ML paper adds four questions: what exactly is claimed, against which baseline, which component carries the gain, and how the plot was drawn. Use invented papers in the house style (a method name, an abstract of four sentences, a results table, an ablation table, a log-log plot), each built for one lesson, plus one real paper per lesson named as further reading.
- **Canvases.** `tap-regions` on an abstract, `table`, `bars`, `plane` on log axes.
- **l1-the-claim** (tags `claim`). Hook: two abstracts for the same invented method; the learner taps the sentence in each that makes a testable claim. Explore: a claim has four parts: a metric, a size, a comparison and a condition ("raises held-out accuracy by 2.1 points over a tuned baseline at 125 million parameters"). Ladder: tap the claim; rewrite "achieves state of the art" into the four parts (`table` with edit cells); spot the condition the abstract leaves out and the paper's body supplies. Trap: "state of the art on twelve benchmarks" read as a strong claim without asking how many were tried (`choice`; L3 returns to it). Edge: a mechanism claim with no headline number (Toy Models: "superposition appears when features are sparse"), whose test is a figure, not a row. Generalise: the three-pass reading (Keshav) as one sentence on top of `momentum-rep` l1's order. Transfer: a fund's research lead asks for the claim in one line before anyone reads further. No code screen is needed; numbers are counts read from the abstracts.
- **l2-baselines** (tags `baseline`, `compute-parity`). Hook: a results table where the method beats the baseline by 2 points; a footnote says the baseline used default settings and one third of the tokens. Explore: a fair baseline gets the same data, the same compute and the same tuning budget (Dodge et al.). Ladder: compute each row's training compute by 6ND: 125 million parameters on 2.5 billion tokens is 1.875 × 10¹⁸ FLOPs, on 1 billion tokens 7.5 × 10¹⁷, a ratio of 2.5 (second method: the code screen); compare tuning trials (20 against 1); decide which comparison the table supports. Trap: "the baseline numbers are copied from the original paper, so they are fair" (different data and code; `choice`). Edge: a baseline that is stronger when retuned than the method (a real outcome in published re-evaluations; describe in words). Generalise: report the tuning budget for every row. Transfer: the same rule for a fund's new signal against the old one, on the same universe and costs. Code screen: `training_flops(params, tokens)` and `parity_table(rows)` returning each row's compute and its ratio to the method's (tests: the two figures above exactly, a ratio of 2.5, and rows sorted by compute).
- **l3-ablations** (tags `ablation`, `interaction`). Hook: a 2 by 2 ablation table: baseline 70.0, with component A 72.0, with B 71.0, with both 75.0; which component matters? Explore: main effects and the interaction. Ladder: gains alone: A 2.0, B 1.0; together 5.0, so the interaction is 75.0 − 72.0 − 71.0 + 70.0 = 2.0 (second method: 5.0 − 2.0 − 1.0); averaged main effects A 3.0 and B 2.0; leave-one-out from the full model costs 4.0 for A and 3.0 for B, which add to 7.0, more than the total gain of 5.0. Trap: adding the leave-one-out costs to explain the whole gain (`numeric` with the slip 7.0 as a `wrong` value). Edge: an ablation run with one seed whose effects are smaller than the seed spread (pointing to L3). Generalise: a table without the interaction cell cannot tell additive from joint effects. Transfer: an ablation table of features in a signal model. Code screen: `ablation_effects(base, a_only, b_only, both)` returning main effects, the interaction and the leave-one-out costs (tests: the figures above, and an additive table whose interaction is 0).
- **l4-plots-on-log-axes** (tags `log-log`, `power-law`). Hook: two points read off a log-log plot of loss against parameters, 1.5849 at 10⁸ and 1.0000 at 10¹⁰; what is the exponent? Explore: on log axes a power law is a straight line, and the slope is the exponent: log₁₀(1.0000/1.5849)/2 = −0.1000 (second method: a fit through three points of L = 10 N^−0.1 gives −0.1000). Ladder: read the slope; then the Chinchilla-shaped curve L = 1.69 + 406.4 N^−0.3392 (constants from Hoffmann et al. 2022, to be checked against the paper), whose values 2.4759 at 10⁸ and 1.8548 at 10¹⁰ give a naive slope of −0.0627; subtracting the irreducible 1.69 first restores −0.3392 (second method for each: `np.polyfit` on the logged points). Trap: fitting a line to log L when the curve has an irreducible term (`choice`: the naive exponent is about a fifth of the true one). Edge: a plot whose axis is labelled in FLOPs while the text talks about parameters; convert with 6ND. Generalise: a fitted exponent always comes with its range of N; never extrapolate it far past the data. Transfer: reading a vendor's plot of "performance against model size" before buying. Code screen: `loglog_slope(x, y)` and `slope_after_offset(x, y, E)` (tests: −0.1 to 1e-9 on the clean law; −0.0627 and −0.3392 to 1e-4 on the curve above).
- **Numbers.** From `papers2.py` (closed form and `np.polyfit` for each slope; 6ND by hand and in code; the ablation sums by two routes, stated above).
- **Homework 1.** H1.1 (`code`, difficulty 5): `extract_claims(table_rows)` takes an invented results table as rows (method, params, tokens, tuning trials, score) and returns the comparisons the table supports: pairs within a factor of 1.25 in compute and with equal tuning budgets, each with its score gap (tests: planted tables with exact answers, including one where the headline pair is excluded). H1.2 (`code`, difficulty 3): `interaction_from_table(df)` on a 2 by 2 ablation in a pandas frame (tests: exact). One `order` item: reading an ML paper (the claim in the abstract; the baseline and its budget; the main table; the ablations; the plots and their axes; the limitations section), with the distractor "read the related-work section first" and its `why`. One `longform` item, 10-point rubric and full model answer: an invented abstract and table claim "our method improves accuracy by 3 points"; the learner writes what the claim needs before it can be believed (compute parity, tuning, seeds, the interaction, the plot's range).

- **Seeded and closed-form figures** (`papers.py`, `papers2.py`, `phase.py`):
  - Allocation exponent a = β/(α + β) = 0.4565. At C = 5.76 × 10²³ FLOPs: N_opt = G (C/6)^a with G = (αA/(βB))^(1/(α+β)) gives 4.031 × 10¹⁰ parameters (second method: a bounded numerical minimisation of A/N^α + B/(C/6N)^β gives the same), D_opt = 2.382 × 10¹² tokens, 59.08 tokens per parameter. With the exponents rounded to 0.34 and 0.28 the same formula gives 3.219 × 10¹⁰ parameters and 92.65 tokens per parameter. The loss law gives 1.9174 at 70 × 10⁹ parameters and 1.4 × 10¹² tokens, and 1.9639 at 280 × 10⁹ and 300 × 10⁹; 6ND is 5.88 × 10²³ and 5.04 × 10²³ for those two (the second method for each loss: the code screen).

Second method: nonlinear least squares on log L from the true point (`scipy.optimize.least_squares`): E 1.6741, A 424.0, B 356.8, α 0.3421, β 0.2771, a 0.4475. A lands 17% above its true 406.4 in the Huber fit and 4% above in the least-squares fit, the two fits' A differ by 12%, and a moves by 0.001 between them: the exponents are identified, the scales much less so.
  - The phase diagram (n = 2 features, m = 1 hidden, importance of feature 1 fixed at 1, of feature 2 r; sparsity S; Adam lr 0.01, batch 1024, 3,000 steps; best of 6 seeds by loss; a feature counts when |w_i| > 0.5, superposed when both count with opposite signs): at S = 0, feature 1 only for r = 0.1, 0.3, 0.7 and feature 2 only for r = 1.5, 3; at S = 0.5, feature 1 only for r = 0.1, 0.3, superposed for 0.7 (4 of 6 seeds) and 1.5 (6 of 6), feature 2 only for 3; at S = 0.8, feature 1 only for 0.1 and superposed elsewhere (0.7 and 1.5 only 3 of 6 seeds); at S = 0.9, superposed everywhere. Second method: the seed counts (each cell's class agrees across the seeds stated). The grid is 120 runs and takes about 100 s natively, so the full grid is supplied as a table and the learner trains single cells.
  - Short runs for graded cases (1,000 steps, batch 256): r = 1.5, S = 0: all four seeds give feature 2 only; r = 0.1, S = 0: feature 1 only for all four; r = 1, S = 0.9: superposed for seeds 0, 2 and 3, not for seed 1. Use seed 0 in tests.
  - The Chinchilla compute at Pyodide's 2.1 GFLOP/s: 2.743 × 10¹⁴ s, about 8.7 million years (by 3.156 × 10⁷ s and by 365.25-day years alike).

## Level 3

Author `ai-papers` level 3 ("Write-up and critique"): `l9-seeds-and-spread`, `l10-error-bars-that-mean-something`, `l11-threats-to-validity`, `l12-the-replication-note`, `review-3.json`, `homework-3.json`, with `docs/reports/ai-papers-3-numbers.py`. Skills: `ap-critique` on all four. Report to `docs/reports/ai-papers-3.md`.

- **Context.** `nn-train` L4 taught a paired comparison across seeds and `post-train` L4 standard errors on benchmarks; this level applies them to reading and writing papers, with new numbers, and adds the reader's view: what a reported error bar does and does not cover. `testing` L3 taught multiple-testing corrections; l11 applies them to many benchmarks in one sentence each and a worked number. The level's written critique is the homework `longform`, self-marked.
- **Figures** (`papers.py`, `papers2.py`; second methods named):
  - Five paired seeds: method A 71.2, 72.5, 70.8, 73.1, 71.9; method B 72.0, 73.6, 71.1, 74.0, 72.3. Means 71.900 and 72.600, difference 0.700; seed sd 0.9354 (A) and 1.1895 (B); sd of the paired differences 0.3391 (a hand loop gives the same). Paired standard error 0.1517, t = 4.616 on 4 degrees of freedom, two-sided p = 0.0099 (second method: `scipy.stats.ttest_rel`). Unpaired (Welch) standard error 0.6768, t = 1.034, p = 0.3328 (`ttest_ind` with `equal_var=False`).
  - Seeds needed to detect a 0.3-point difference at 5% two-sided with 80% power, using the paired sd 0.3391: ((1.96 + 0.84) × 0.3391/0.3)² = 10.03, so 11 seeds; for 0.5 points, 3.61, so 4 (second method: a search for the smallest n with normal-approximation power of at least 0.8 gives 11 and 4).
  - Items: two models on the same 1,000 questions at 72% and 74%; A right and B wrong on 40, the reverse on 60. Difference 0.0200; paired standard error √(100 − 20²/1000)/1000 = 0.009980 (second method: the sd of the per-item differences over √n); unpaired 0.019849; z = 2.004 paired against 1.008 unpaired. One accuracy of 72% has standard error 0.0142 on 1,000 items and 0.0317 on 200.
  - Many benchmarks: twenty independent tests at 5% give at least one false win with probability 1 − 0.95²⁰ = 0.6415; the Bonferroni level is 0.0025.
  - The best of five seeds: the expected maximum of five standard normals is 1.16296 (numerical integration; simulation of 400,000 draws gives 1.16342), so reporting the best seed inflates a result by about 1.16 seed sds.
- **Canvases.** `bars` with intervals (per-seed results), `distribution` (the maximum of five draws against one), `table` (discordant counts), `scatter` (paired seeds: A against B, points above the diagonal).
- **l9-seeds-and-spread** (tags `seed-variance`). Hook: method B beats A on four of five seeds by under a point; the learner guesses whether that is evidence.
