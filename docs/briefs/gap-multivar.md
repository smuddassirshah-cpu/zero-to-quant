- l17 (change of variables): hook: the diamond $|x| + |y| \le 1$ is awkward in $x$ and $y$ but is a square in $u = x + y$, $v = x - y$; the learner drags a small square in the $(u, v)$ plane and watches its image, a smaller tilted square, on a `pair` of planes; the image's area is half; the area factor named the **Jacobian**: for $x = x(u, v)$, $y = y(u, v)$ a $du \times dv$ cell maps to a parallelogram with sides $(x_u, y_u)\,du$ and $(x_v, y_v)\,dv$, whose area is $|x_u y_v - x_v y_u|\,du\,dv$ (derived on a `plane` as rectangle minus triangles; a justification per line). Ladder: here $x = \tfrac{u + v}{2}$, $y = \tfrac{u - v}{2}$, so $x_u y_v - x_v y_u = -\tfrac12$ and $|J| = \tfrac12$; $\iint_{|x| + |y| \le 1}(x + y)^2\,dA = \int_{-1}^1\int_{-1}^1 u^2 \cdot \tfrac12\,du\,dv = \tfrac23$ (checked directly in $x$ and $y$ too); polar as a change of variables: $x = r\cos\theta$, $y = r\sin\theta$ gives $|J| = r$, the l15 cell; a stretch $x = 2u$, $y = 3v$ turns the unit disc into an ellipse of area $6\pi$. Trap: forgetting the absolute value (the sign of $J$ flips with the order of $u$ and $v$; an area cannot be negative), or using $J$ for $u, v$ in terms of $x, y$ where $x, y$ in terms of $u, v$ is needed (they are reciprocals: $|\partial(u, v)/\partial(x, y)| = 2$ here). Edge case: a map that folds the plane ($u = x^2$) is not one-to-one, so the formula needs the region split. Transfer: two daily returns $X$ and $Y$ with joint density $f(x, y)$; a desk trades the sum $U = X + Y$ and the spread $V = X - Y$; the joint density of $(U, V)$ is $\tfrac12 f\left(\tfrac{u + v}{2}, \tfrac{u - v}{2}\right)$; for the l15 density $\tfrac{1}{\pi}e^{-(x^2 + y^2)}$ it becomes $\tfrac{1}{2\pi}e^{-(u^2 + v^2)/2}$, which still integrates to 1 and factors, so the sum and the spread are independent here (state it; topic 1.5 proves the general rule). One sentence: Vectors and Matrices calls $ad - bc$ a determinant (vectors l10).
- l18 (triple integrals): hook: three independent waits, each uniform on $[0, 1]$; what is the chance their total is under 1? The learner slides a slice height $z$ on a `plane` showing the triangle $x + y \le 1 - z$ shrinking, and adds slice areas $\tfrac12(1 - z)^2$; the answer $\tfrac16$ is the volume of a tetrahedron; the triple integral named as one more layer of iteration, $\iiint_E f\,dV$. Ladder: a box, $\iiint_{[0,1]\times[0,2]\times[0,3]} xyz\,dV = \tfrac12 \cdot 2 \cdot \tfrac92 = 4.5$ (the product splits, as l13's did); the tetrahedron with limits in the order $z$, then $y$, then $x$; cylindrical coordinates in one screen ($dV = r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz$, the polar cell stacked); spherical coordinates, $dV = \rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta$, with the volume of the unit ball $\tfrac43\pi$; the three-dimensional bell, $\iiint e^{-(x^2 + y^2 + z^2)}\,dV = \pi^{3/2} \approx 5.5683$, from l16's $\sqrt{\pi}$ cubed. Trap: limits that use the outer variable in the wrong layer (the innermost limits may depend on the outer variables, never the reverse); dropping the $\rho^2\sin\phi$. Edge case: a solid whose shadow must be split into two regions. `code` screen: `mc_volume(n, seed)` estimates the tetrahedron's volume by drawing `n` seeded points in the unit cube with `random.Random(seed)` and returning the share with $x + y + z \le 1$ (tests: exact on fixed seeds against your CPython values; a loose `isclose` to $\tfrac16$ at $n = 200{,}000$ with a tolerance you justify; keep the wall time under a second), with a `scatter` of the points. Transfer: a risk limit on three uncorrelated desks' daily P&L, each with density $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}e^{-x^2}$: the chance the combined move $\sqrt{X^2 + Y^2 + Z^2}$ exceeds 1.5, in spherical coordinates (compute and report; the 2D version in l15 gave a different number, and the learner predicts which is larger first).
- l19 (Lagrange with several constraints): hook: the closest point of the plane $x + 2y + 3z = 14$ to the origin; the learner moves a point on the plane's trace and watches the distance; the conditions $\nabla f = \lambda\nabla g$ in three variables give $(x, y, z) = \tfrac{\lambda}{2}(1, 2, 3)$, so the point is $(1, 2, 3)$ and $\min(x^2 + y^2 + z^2) = 14$ ($\lambda = 2$). Ladder: two constraints, $\nabla f = \lambda\nabla g + \mu\nabla h$, on a small example (the closest point to the origin on the line where two planes meet; choose one with whole-number answers and check it); three uncorrelated assets with vols 20%, 30% and 40% under the budget alone: weights proportional to $\tfrac{1}{\sigma_i^2}$, $w = (0.5902, 0.2623, 0.1475)$, portfolio variance $\tfrac{1}{\sum 1/\sigma_i^2} = 0.02361$, vol 15.36%; then add a target return with expected returns 5%, 8% and 11%: the two-constraint system for a target of 8% gives $w = \left(\tfrac{9}{28}, \tfrac{10}{28}, \tfrac{9}{28}\right)$ with variance $\tfrac{9}{280} \approx 0.03214$ (vol 17.93%); the learner fills the linear system in a `table` or `matrix` and solves it. Trap: solving $\nabla f = \lambda\nabla g$ but forgetting one constraint equation, or comparing only one candidate when there are several; reading a negative weight as an error rather than a short position (state the no-short-sales clip from l12 and leave the constrained case to l20). Edge case: constraints that are inconsistent (parallel planes) or redundant (the same plane twice): no solution, or a free multiplier. Generalise: the count of unknowns and equations ($n$ variables and $m$ constraints give $n + m$ of each) as in l11's generalise screen. Derive: the minimum-variance weights for uncorrelated assets, $w_i = \tfrac{1/\sigma_i^2}{\sum_j 1/\sigma_j^2}$, from the Lagrange conditions, a justification per line. Transfer: the efficient frontier: repeat for targets 6%, 7%, 8% and 9% and plot portfolio vol against target on a `plane` (compute and report each point; the minimum-variance portfolio's own return is 6.67%); the multiplier on the return constraint is the extra variance per unit of extra target return, the frontier's slope.
- l20 (extremes on a closed region): hook: $f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2 - 2x$ on the disc $x^2 + y^2 \le 4$; the learner taps where they think the largest value is; the interior has one stationary point, $(1, 0)$ with $f = -1$, a minimum; on the boundary $x^2 + y^2 = 4$ the function is $4 - 2x$, largest at $(-2, 0)$ with $f = 8$ and smallest at $(2, 0)$ with $f = 0$; so the absolute maximum is 8 on the edge and the absolute minimum is $-1$ inside; the method named: a continuous function on a closed, bounded region has both (the extreme value theorem in two variables); check the interior stationary points, then the boundary (by parametrising it, or by Lagrange from l11), then compare every candidate in a `table`. Ladder: a triangle region (three edges, three corners: corners are candidates too); a square of position sizes, maximising the mean-variance score $2w_1 + w_2 - (w_1^2 + w_2^2 + w_1 w_2)$ on $0 \le w_1, w_2 \le 0.6$: the stationary point $(1, 0)$ lies outside the square, and the best point is $(0.6, 0.2)$ on the edge $w_1 = 0.6$, with score 0.88 (not a corner: the corner $(0.6, 0)$ scores 0.84). Trap: reporting the interior stationary point as the maximum (it is the minimum here); checking the edges but not the corners. Edge case: an open region with no maximum ($x^2 + y^2$ on the open disc). Transfer: a long-only fund with a 50% position limit per asset in the l19 three-asset example at an 8% target (weights are already within limits; then a 10% target, where the unconstrained solution is $w = (-0.083, 0.5, 0.583)$, short the first asset and over the limit in the third, so the optimum moves to the boundary: compute and report it), the way an optimiser handles box constraints.
