# Level brief: testing (Hypothesis Testing, topic 1.6), gap level 4

Course context. `testing` is the second of the three statistics courses of topic 1.6 (estimation, testing, regression). Levels 1 to 3 are written and reviewed (null and alternative, $z$ and $t$ statistics, p-values, the two errors, power and sample size, two groups, multiple testing, the Sharpe ratio test, data snooping). The self-containment review (`docs/reviews/critical-review-fable-5-2/self-containment-gaps.md`, row 1.6) found the theory a statistics course carries missing from the whole topic: the method of moments, sufficiency, Fisher information and the Cramér–Rao bound, and likelihood-ratio tests have no lesson, and chi-squared tests are mentioned (in `regression/l6`, `arma/l6`, `garch`, `stat-models/l5`) but never taught. Level 4 adds all six.

Why this course and not `estimation`. Four of the six lessons are estimation theory, so `estimation` level 4 was the other candidate. The `requires` edges decide it. A level may use only what its prerequisites teach, and the last two lessons need hypotheses, size, power and p-values (`testing` levels 1 and 2), while `testing/L1` itself requires `estimation/L3`. Placing the level in `estimation` would make the last level of the first statistics course wait on the second course, with `regression/L1` (which requires `estimation/L1`) sitting between them on the Learn page. In `testing` every prerequisite is upstream: `testing/L2` (errors and power; level 3 on multiple testing is not used, so it is not required), which brings `estimation` levels 1 to 3 through `testing/L1`, and `limits-thm/L4` for the chi-square, Gamma and $t$ distributions and joint densities (`stat-models/L3` already requires that level). The cost is that the course's four skills are all about tests; see the skills note below. The `course.json` edits are in `docs/briefs/gap-syllabus-holes-course-json.md`.

- l14 (sufficiency): hook: two traders each record 10 trades; one sheet reads win, win, loss, …, the other only "7 wins"; the learner writes the likelihood of the full sequence, $p^7(1 - p)^3$, and of any other order of 7 wins: the same function of $p$. On a `grid` of the $\binom{10}{7} = 120$ orders, each has chance $\tfrac{1}{120}$ given 7 wins, whatever $p$ is: once the count is known, the order says nothing more about $p$. **Sufficient statistic** named, with the definition by conditioning, then the working tool, the **factorisation theorem**: $T$ is sufficient exactly when the joint density factors as $g(T(x), \theta)\,h(x)$. Ladder: the learner factorises: exponential gaps ($\lambda^n e^{-\lambda\sum x_i}$: the total), the normal mean with known $\sigma$ (the sample mean), the normal with both unknown (the pair $\sum x_i$, $\sum x_i^2$), the uniform on $(0, \theta)$ (the largest observation, through an indicator: link l13); the MLE is a function of a sufficient statistic whenever both exist (one sentence, with the coin). Then why it matters: conditioning an unbiased estimator on a sufficient statistic never increases its variance (Rao–Blackwell, stated with its hypotheses and shown on one case): the first trade's result is unbiased for $p$ with variance $p(1 - p)$; its conditional mean given the count is $\tfrac{k}{n}$, with variance $\tfrac{p(1 - p)}{n}$: at $p = 0.6$, $n = 10$, 0.24 against 0.024. Trap: calling any summary sufficient (the first observation alone, or the sample mean for the uniform, loses information: show two samples with the same mean and different maxima); thinking sufficiency depends on the data rather than the model (the count is sufficient for independent trades with one $p$, not if $p$ drifts). Edge case: the whole sample is always sufficient, so the aim is the smallest such summary (minimal sufficiency in one sentence, no theory). Transfer: a risk system stores, for each day, only the number of returns, their sum and their sum of squares; for a normal model nothing is lost and both parameters can be re-estimated exactly, but a fat-tail parameter cannot, because that model's sufficient statistic is not those three numbers; the learner decides which of four questions the stored numbers can still answer.
- l15 (Fisher information): hook: 7 wins in 10 trades and 70 in 100: the two log-likelihoods on one `plane`, scaled to the same peak at $\hat p = 0.7$; the learner drags a point off the peak and sees the second fall away ten times as fast. The slope of the log-likelihood named the **score**; its mean is 0 at the true parameter; its variance is the **Fisher information**, which also equals minus the expected second derivative (the curvature the learner just saw). For one Bernoulli trial $I(p) = \tfrac{1}{p(1 - p)}$, and for $n$ independent trials $n$ times that: at $p = 0.7$, 4.7619 per trade and 476.19 for 100 trades. Ladder: the learner computes $I$ for the normal mean with known $\sigma$ ($\tfrac{1}{\sigma^2}$), the exponential rate ($\tfrac{1}{\lambda^2}$) and the Poisson mean ($\tfrac{1}{\lambda}$); the large-sample fact, stated with its hypotheses (independent observations, a smooth model whose support does not depend on the parameter, the true value inside the range): the MLE is approximately normal with variance $\tfrac{1}{nI(\theta)}$, so its standard error is $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{nI(\hat\theta)}}$: $\sqrt{0.21/100} = 0.0458$ for the hit rate, the formula of estimation l9 recovered; the debt from estimation l8: for a normal with known mean, $I(\sigma) = \tfrac{2}{\sigma^2}$, so the standard error of $\hat\sigma$ is $\tfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt{2n}}$: with $\sigma = 1\%$ a day and 252 days, 0.0445 percentage points, a relative error of 4.45%. Trap: information is per parameterisation: $I(\sigma^2) = \tfrac{1}{2\sigma^4}$ is not $I(\sigma)$ (the learner converts one to the other with the chain rule); adding information across observations that are not independent (overlapping returns); reading a sharp peak as proof the model is right. Edge case: the uniform on $(0, \theta)$ breaks the hypotheses (its support moves with $\theta$): the score's mean is not 0 and the MLE's error shrinks like $\tfrac{1}{n}$, not $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$. - l16 (the Cramér–Rao bound): hook: three unbiased estimators of a normal mean from 101 observations with $\sigma = 1$: the sample mean, the sample median, and the average of the first and last observation; a seeded `bars` `sim` shows their spreads; the learner ranks them and asks whether anything could beat the mean. The bound named: for an unbiased estimator in a regular model (l15's hypotheses), $\mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta) \ge \tfrac{1}{nI(\theta)}$; here $\tfrac{\sigma^2}{n}$, which the sample mean attains exactly: it is **efficient**. The median's variance is about $\tfrac{\pi}{2}\cdot\tfrac{\sigma^2}{n}$, so its efficiency is $\tfrac{2}{\pi} \approx 0.6366$: it needs about 57% more data (the factor $\tfrac{\pi}{2} \approx 1.5708$) for the same precision. Ladder: the hit rate: the bound is $\tfrac{p(1 - p)}{n}$ and $\tfrac{k}{n}$ attains it; the exponential rate: the MLE $\tfrac{1}{\bar x}$ is biased ($E = \tfrac{n\lambda}{n - 1}$: 2.2222 for $\lambda = 2$, $n = 10$); the unbiased version $\tfrac{n - 1}{\sum x_i}$ has variance $\tfrac{\lambda^2}{n - 2} = 0.5$ against a bound of $\tfrac{\lambda^2}{n} = 0.4$: not attained at any finite $n$, attained in the limit; the sample variance $s^2$ of a normal: variance $\tfrac{2\sigma^4}{n - 1}$ against a bound of $\tfrac{2\sigma^4}{n}$ ($\tfrac{2}{9}$ against $\tfrac{2}{10}$ at $n = 10$, $\sigma = 1$). Trap: applying the bound to a biased estimator: dividing the sum of squares by $n + 1$ has lower MSE than the bound for unbiased ones (estimation l4), and that is no contradiction; reading "attains the bound" as "is the MLE" or the reverse. Edge case: the uniform again: the unbiased $\tfrac{n + 1}{n}\max x_i$ has variance $\tfrac{\theta^2}{n(n + 2)}$ ($\tfrac{1}{120}$ at $n = 10$, $\theta = 1$), far below the $\tfrac{1}{n}$ rate, because the model is not regular and the bound does not apply. Derive (the lesson's `order` screens, in two parts): $\mathrm{Cov}(\hat\theta, \text{score}) = 1$ for an unbiased estimator, by differentiating $E[\hat\theta] = \theta$ under the integral; then $|\rho| \le 1$ between $\hat\theta$ and the score (random-vars l11; the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality of random-vars l19 is the same fact) gives $1 \le \mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta)\,nI(\theta)$; a justification per line. Transfer: volatility is easy and the mean is hard: for daily returns with mean 0.04% and sd 1%, the standard error of the mean is $\tfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$, so a $t$ statistic of 2 needs $n = (2 \times 1/0.04)^2 = 2{,}500$ days, about 9.9 years, while the volatility is known to 5% after 200 days (l15); no estimator can do better than these bounds from the same data, which is why desks size risk from recent data and treat expected returns with suspicion.
- l17 (likelihood-ratio tests): hook: a strategy's 20 trades (testing l3); is the hit rate 0.5 or 0.6? For $k$ wins the **likelihood ratio** is $\tfrac{0.6^k\,0.4^{20 - k}}{0.5^{20}} = 1.2^k\,0.8^{20 - k}$: 0.665 at 10 wins, 5.049 at 15; the learner sees on a `pair` of binomials that it rises with $k$, so "reject when the ratio is large" is "reject when $k$ is large". The **Neyman–Pearson lemma** named: for two simple hypotheses, among all tests of the same size the likelihood-ratio test has the greatest power. With the rule $k \ge 15$ the size is 0.0207 (14 would give 0.0577, over 5%) and the power at 0.6 is 0.1256; a rival test of the same size, "reject when $k \le 5$", has power 0.0016, and "reject when the first six trades all win" has size 0.0156 and power 0.0467. Ladder: when the alternative is not one value, the **generalised** ratio compares the best fit under the null with the best fit overall, $\Lambda = \tfrac{\max_{H_0} L}{\max L}$, and for large samples from a regular model $-2\ln\Lambda$ is approximately chi-squared with as many degrees of freedom as the null fixes parameters (Wilks, stated with its hypotheses: the null value inside the parameter range, independent observations, large $n$). Poisson trade counts: 50 minutes with 120 trades, null $\lambda = 2$: $\hat\lambda = 2.4$ and $-2\ln\Lambda = 2\,[120\ln 1.2 - 50 \times 0.4] = 3.757$, below the 5% cut-off 3.841 (p = 0.0526); the same data by a $z$ statistic with the null variance give $z = 2.0$ (p = 0.0455) and with the estimated variance $z = 1.826$ (p = 0.0679): three tests that agree in large samples and can disagree at the margin; for a normal mean with known $\sigma$, $-2\ln\Lambda = z^2$ exactly (the learner shows it), so the $z$ test of l2 is a likelihood-ratio test. Two nested models: is volatility the same in two regimes? 100 days each, mean taken as 0, MLE variances 1.00 and 1.69 (%²): pooled 1.345, $-2\ln\Lambda = 100\ln 1.345 + 100\ln\tfrac{1.345}{1.69} = 6.806$ on 1 degree of freedom, p = 0.0091. Edge case: non-nested models (a normal against a $t$) have no chi-squared limit.
