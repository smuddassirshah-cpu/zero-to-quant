# Level brief: quadratics (Quadratics, topic 0.2), gap level 4

Course context. `quadratics` is the third of the three algebra courses of topic 0.2 (equations, functions, quadratics). Levels 1 to 3 are written and reviewed (expanding two brackets, factorising with and without a leading coefficient, the difference of two squares, solving by factorising, completing the square, the formula, the discriminant, parabolas, turning points, roots on a sketch, quadratic inequalities, a revenue model). The self-containment review (`docs/reviews/critical-review-fable-5-2/self-containment-gaps.md`, row 0.2) found five things with no lesson anywhere in topic 0.2: algebraic fractions, surds and rationalising, polynomial division and the factor theorem, circles in coordinates, and a line meeting a curve beyond one screen. Level 4 adds all five.

Why this course. Every one of the five needs factorising (quadratics level 1) and four need solving a quadratic or the discriminant (level 2); `equations` level 4 and `functions` level 4 come before or beside quadratics level 2 in the `requires` graph, so neither can hold them. `quadratics` level 4 can use everything: it requires `quadratics/L3` (which already brings `functions/L3`, and so gradients and perpendicular lines from `functions/L2`), `equations/L4` (substituting a line into a curve, `l15-swapping-in`) and the new `number-sense/L4` (prime factors and the HCF, for surds and for cancelling). Surds are placed here, as l14, not in `number-sense` level 4: no lesson of topic 0.1 or 0.2 teaches them, and their uses (exact roots from the formula, rationalising, $5\sqrt{2}$ as a tangency condition) are all in this course. The `course.json` edits are in `docs/briefs/gap-syllabus-holes-course-json.md`.

- l14 (surds): hook: on a dot `grid`, a tilted square of area 2 and one of area 8; the learner counts the areas, so the sides are $\sqrt{2}$ and $\sqrt{8}$, then sees that the larger side is exactly two of the smaller: $\sqrt{8} = 2\sqrt{2}$. A **surd** named: a root that does not come out as a whole number or fraction, kept exact. The rule the picture showed: $\sqrt{ab} = \sqrt{a}\sqrt{b}$, so pull out the largest square factor (prime factors, number-sense l15): $\sqrt{72} = \sqrt{36}\sqrt{2} = 6\sqrt{2}$. Ladder: like surds collect as like terms do, $\sqrt{50} + \sqrt{18} = 5\sqrt{2} + 3\sqrt{2} = 8\sqrt{2}$; brackets: $(1 + \sqrt{3})^2 = 4 + 2\sqrt{3}$ and $(3 + \sqrt{2})(3 - \sqrt{2}) = 7$ (the difference of two squares, l4, makes the surd vanish); **rationalising**: $\tfrac{6}{\sqrt{3}} = 2\sqrt{3}$ by multiplying top and bottom by $\sqrt{3}$, and $\tfrac{1}{3 - \sqrt{2}} = \tfrac{3 + \sqrt{2}}{7}$ with the conjugate; exact roots: $x^2 - 6x + 1 = 0$ gives $x = \tfrac{6 \pm \sqrt{32}}{2} = 3 \pm 2\sqrt{2}$, about 5.828 and 0.172 (the learner divides both terms by 2: l7's trap again). Trap: $\sqrt{a + b}$ is not $\sqrt{a} + \sqrt{b}$ ($\sqrt{9 + 16} = 5$, not 7); stopping at $\sqrt{72} = 2\sqrt{18}$ or $3\sqrt{8}$ (a square factor is still inside); halving only one term of $\tfrac{6 \pm \sqrt{32}}{2}$. Edge case: $\sqrt{a} \times \sqrt{a} = a$ only for $a \ge 0$, and a decimal such as 1.414 is an approximation, not $\sqrt{2}$. Derive (an `order` screen): why $\sqrt{a}\sqrt{b} = \sqrt{ab}$ for positive $a$ and $b$ (square both sides; both are positive; a justification per line). Transfer: risk grows with the square root of time (stated, as `number-sense/l11` did): a share with daily volatility 1% has annual volatility $\sqrt{252}\% = 6\sqrt{7}\% \approx 15.87\%$ over 252 trading days; the learner simplifies $\sqrt{252}$ from $252 = 2^2 \times 3^2 \times 7$ and compares it with the common slip of multiplying by 252.
- l15 (simplifying, multiplying and dividing algebraic fractions): hook: the learner simplifies $\tfrac{84}{120}$ by cancelling the common factor 12, then meets $\tfrac{x^2 + 5x + 6}{x^2 + 3x + 2}$ and tries $x = 1$ ($\tfrac{12}{6} = 2$) and two more values in a `table` beside $\tfrac{x + 3}{x + 1}$: the columns agree. Factorising shows why: $\tfrac{(x + 2)(x + 3)}{(x + 1)(x + 2)}$, and the common **factor** $(x + 2)$ cancels. Rule named: factorise top and bottom fully, then cancel factors, never terms. Ladder: a common factor first ($\tfrac{x^2 - 9}{x^2 + 3x} = \tfrac{x - 3}{x}$); multiplying, cancelling across before multiplying: $\tfrac{x^2 - 4}{x + 3} \times \tfrac{2x + 6}{x - 2} = 2(x + 2)$; dividing by flipping the second fraction (number-sense l5), with one example the learner sets (compute and report); a fraction with a single term below splits, $\tfrac{x^2 + 3x}{x} = x + 3$. Trap: cancelling terms: $\tfrac{x + 6}{x + 2}$ is not 3 (try $x = 1$: $\tfrac{7}{3}$), and $\tfrac{x^2 + 3x}{x}$ is not $x^2 + 3$; cancelling $x$ from one term only. Edge case: the simplified form forgets where the original was undefined: $\tfrac{x^2 + 5x + 6}{x^2 + 3x + 2}$ has no value at $x = -2$ or $x = -1$, while $\tfrac{x + 3}{x + 1}$ gives $-1$ at $x = -2$ (a `plane` with a hole at $(-2, -1)$; link `functions/l3` on domains). Transfer: a return is $\tfrac{p_1 - p_0}{p_0} = \tfrac{p_1}{p_0} - 1$: the single term below lets the fraction split, which is why a price ratio of 1.05 is a 5% return; the reverse split, $\tfrac{p_0}{p_1 - p_0}$, is not allowed (the learner checks with £80 and £84).
- l16 (adding algebraic fractions and solving with them): hook: you put £600 into a share at £$p$ and another £600 a month later at £$(p + 1)$, and hold 500 shares in all: $\tfrac{600}{p} + \tfrac{600}{p + 1} = 500$, or $\tfrac{6}{p} + \tfrac{6}{p + 1} = 5$ in hundreds; the learner tries $p = 1$, 2 and 3 in a `table` and finds $p = 2$ (300 shares, then 200). Then the method that needs no guessing: a common denominator, as with $\tfrac{5}{12} + \tfrac{7}{18}$ (number-sense l16), here $p(p + 1)$: $\tfrac{1}{x} + \tfrac{1}{x + 1} = \tfrac{2x + 1}{x(x + 1)}$. Ladder: subtracting, with the bracket and the minus sign, $\tfrac{3}{x - 1} - \tfrac{2}{x + 2} = \tfrac{x + 8}{(x - 1)(x + 2)}$; solving by multiplying every term by the common denominator (equations l8): $6(x + 1) + 6x = 5x(x + 1)$, so $5x^2 - 7x - 6 = 0$, $(5x + 3)(x - 2) = 0$, $x = 2$ or $x = -\tfrac{3}{5}$, and only 2 is a price; a denominator that is a quadratic to factorise first (one example, compute and report). Trap: adding tops and adding bottoms ($\tfrac{1}{x} + \tfrac{1}{x + 1}$ is not $\tfrac{2}{2x + 1}$: try $x = 1$); losing the minus on the second term of a subtracted top; multiplying only some terms by the common denominator. Edge case: an answer that makes a denominator zero is no answer: $\tfrac{x}{x - 2} = \tfrac{2}{x - 2} + 3$ leads to $x = 2$, which the equation forbids, so it has no solution (always check in the original). Derive (an `order` screen): $\tfrac{a}{b} + \tfrac{c}{d} = \tfrac{ad + bc}{bd}$ line by line. Transfer: the average price you paid in the hook is $\tfrac{1{,}200}{500} = £2.40$, below the £2.50 midpoint of the two prices, because equal pounds buy more shares when the price is low; in letters the average for prices $a$ and $b$ is $\tfrac{2ab}{a + b}$ (the learner derives it by adding $\tfrac{1}{a} + \tfrac{1}{b}$).
- l17 (polynomial division and the factor theorem): hook: $f(x) = x^3 - 2x^2 - 5x + 6$ on a `plane`; the learner fills a `table` of $f(1)$, $f(2)$, $f(3)$ and $f(-2)$ and finds 0, $-4$, 0 and 0: the curve crosses the axis at 1, 3 and $-2$, and (level 2, l5) a product is zero when a bracket is zero, so $(x - 1)$, $(x - 3)$ and $(x + 2)$ should be factors. Division confirms it: $(x^3 - 2x^2 - 5x + 6) \div (x - 1) = x^2 - x - 6 = (x - 3)(x + 2)$, set out beside the long division $252 \div 12 = 21$ so each step has a number twin (a `table` or the `area` grid method run backwards). The **factor theorem** named: $(x - a)$ is a factor exactly when $f(a) = 0$; and the **remainder theorem**: otherwise $f(a)$ is the remainder ($f(2) = -4$ is what is left on dividing by $x - 2$). Ladder: a division with a remainder, $(2x^3 + 3x^2 - 4x + 5) \div (x + 2)$: quotient $2x^2 - x - 2$, remainder 9, checked by $f(-2) = 9$; factorising a cubic from scratch by trying the factors of the constant term (number-sense l15): $x^3 - 7x + 6 = (x - 1)(x - 2)(x + 3)$; finding a coefficient: $(x - 2)$ is a factor of $x^3 + kx^2 - 4$, so $8 + 4k - 4 = 0$ and $k = -1$. Trap: the sign: dividing by $(x + 2)$ tests $f(-2)$, not $f(2)$; leaving no column for a missing term ($x^3 - 7x + 6$ has $0x^2$). Edge case: a repeated factor, $x^3 - 3x + 2 = (x - 1)^2(x + 2)$, where the curve touches the axis; and a cubic with one real root, $x^3 + x - 2 = (x - 1)(x^2 + x + 2)$, whose quadratic factor has discriminant $-7$ (l8). Derive (an `order` screen): the remainder theorem from $f(x) = (x - a)q(x) + r$ with $x = a$ put in, a justification per line. Transfer: you invest £1,000 and receive £400, £480 and £576 at the end of years 1, 2 and 3; the yearly growth factor $x$ that makes the payments worth exactly the cost satisfies $1{,}000x^3 - 400x^2 - 480x - 576 = 0$; the learner checks that $x = 1.2$ is a root (a 20% a year return), divides out to get $125x^3 - 50x^2 - 60x - 72 = (5x - 6)(25x^2 + 20x + 12)$, and uses the discriminant ($-800$) to show there is no other real rate.

- l19 (a line meeting a curve): hook: the circle $x^2 + y^2 = 25$ and the line $y = x + k$ with a slider on $k$; the learner slides it and counts meeting points: two, then one at the edge, then none; at $k = 1$ they find the points by substitution: $x^2 + (x + 1)^2 = 25$ gives $x^2 + x - 12 = 0$, so $(3, 4)$ and $(-4, -3)$.
