**Notation for the whole course (fix it in l1 and l2 and keep it).** A group is $(G, \ast)$ or just $G$, with identity $e$ (written $0$ in additive groups such as $\mathbb{Z}_n$, and $1$ in multiplicative ones) and inverse $g^{-1}$ (or $-g$). $|G|$ is the order of the group and $\operatorname{ord}(g)$ the order of an element; say once that Pinter writes $\operatorname{ord}(a)$ too. Composition is written as a product and read right to left: $rs$ means "do $s$, then $r$", exactly as $\mathbf{AB}$ in `vectors` l7 applies $\mathbf{B}$ first. The square's symmetries form $D_4$ (8 elements; say once that some books write $D_8$): $r$ is the anticlockwise quarter turn and $s$ the flip in the vertical axis, with matrices $r = \begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ and $s = \begin{pmatrix}-1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$. The eight elements are $e, r, r^2, r^3, s, rs, r^2s, r^3s$. Corners are numbered 1 at $(1, 1)$, 2 at $(-1, 1)$, 3 at $(-1, -1)$ and 4 at $(1, -1)$. Then $r = (1\,2\,3\,4)$ and $s = (1\,2)(3\,4)$ as permutations of the corners, and the relation is $sr = r^3s$. The triangle's group is $D_3$, with $r$ the turn by $120°$. Residues: $\mathbb{Z}_n = \{0, 1, \dots, n - 1\}$, $a \equiv b \pmod n$, $a \bmod n$ (as in `contests` l5), the units $\mathbb{Z}_n^{\times}$ and Euler's $\varphi(n) = |\mathbb{Z}_n^{\times}|$. Cyclic subgroup $\langle g \rangle$; $\langle a, b \rangle$ for two generators. Roots of unity $\mu_n = \{e^{2\pi ik/n}\}$ with $\zeta = e^{2\pi i/n}$; never use $i$ as an index (item 446), so exponents use $k$ or $j$. Cosets $gH$ (left) and $Hg$ (right), index $[G : H]$, $N \trianglelefteq G$, quotient $G/N$. Permutations in two-line notation and in cycle notation $(1\,5\,4)(2\,3\,8)$, with each cycle starting at its smallest entry and fixed points left out; $S_n$, $A_n$, $\operatorname{sgn}$. Homomorphism $\varphi$, $\ker\varphi$, $\operatorname{im}\varphi$, $\cong$. Rings $R$ with unity $1$ (say once that Pinter does not require a unity, so for him $2\mathbb{Z}$ is a ring); fields $\mathbb{F}$, $\mathbb{F}_p = \mathbb{Z}_p$, $\mathbb{F}_4$; polynomials $f(x) \in \mathbb{F}[x]$ with $\deg f$. - l15 (fields and finite fields). Hook: two multiplication tables side by side, $\mathbb{Z}_7$ and $\mathbb{Z}_6$, on a `pair` of `grid` canvases. The learner taps the 1 in each non-zero row of $\mathbb{Z}_7$: every row has one, so every non-zero element has an inverse ($2 \leftrightarrow 4$, $3 \leftrightarrow 5$, $6 \leftrightarrow 6$; `table`). In $\mathbb{Z}_6$ the rows of 2, 3 and 4 have no 1, and $2 \times 3 = 0$. Name integral domain (commutative, $1 \ne 0$, no zero divisors) and field (every non-zero element a unit). Proofs and widgets: (a) $\mathbb{Z}_p$ is a field for a prime $p$: for $0 < a < p$, $\gcd(a, p) = 1$, so $a$ has an inverse by l3 (`order`; `choice` for why the gcd is 1: the only divisors of $p$ are 1 and $p$). (b) A finite integral domain is a field. This is the `derive` screen, an `order`: fix $a \ne 0$; the map $x \mapsto ax$ is injective, since $ax = ay$ gives $a(x - y) = 0$ and so $x = y$; an injective map of a finite set to itself is onto; so some $x$ has $ax = 1$. Its distractor is "the map is onto because it is a homomorphism", whose `why` says onto comes from finiteness. (c) $\mathbb{Z}_n$ for composite $n = ab$ with $1 < a, b < n$ is not a field: $ab = 0$ makes $a$ a zero divisor, and a unit is never one (l14) (`order`). Ladder: which $\mathbb{Z}_n$ are fields for $n$ from 2 to 12 (`multi`: 2, 3, 5, 7, 11); solve $3x + 4 \equiv 2 \pmod 7$ by multiplying by $3^{-1} = 5$ (`numeric`: compute and confirm, $x = 4$); $\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{R}$ and $\mathbb{C}$ are fields, $\mathbb{Z}$ is an integral domain but not a field (`choice`). Trap: "$\mathbb{Z}_4$ is the field with four elements". In $\mathbb{Z}_4$, $2 \times 2 = 0$. The field with four elements is $\mathbb{F}_4 = \{0, 1, \alpha, \alpha + 1\}$ with $\alpha^2 = \alpha + 1$, where $\alpha(\alpha + 1) = 1$ and $(\alpha + 1)^2 = \alpha$ (`grid` multiplication table of $\mathbb{F}_4$ beside $\mathbb{Z}_4$'s; `choice`). Say that finite fields have prime power sizes, as a fact (Pinter chapter 27 has more). Edge case: $\mathbb{Z}_2$ is the smallest field; in a field of characteristic $p$, $1 + 1 + \cdots + 1$ ($p$ times) is 0. Transfer: sharing a secret key. A firm splits a signing key so that any 2 of 3 desk heads can recover it but one alone cannot. Over $\mathbb{Z}_{11}$, pick the line $f(x) = 7 + 3x$ with the secret $f(0) = 7$, and hand out the shares $(1, 10)$, $(2, 2)$ and $(3, 5)$ (`table`). From $(2, 2)$ and $(3, 5)$ the slope is $(5 - 2)/(3 - 2) = 3$ and the secret is $2 - 2 \times 3 \equiv 7$ (`numeric`); from $(1, 10)$ and $(3, 5)$ the slope needs $2^{-1} = 6$ (`numeric` for the slope, 3). Over $\mathbb{Z}_{10}$ the scheme breaks: the shares $(1, 0)$ and $(3, 6)$ fit both $7 + 3x$ and $2 + 8x$, so the secret could be 7 or 2, because 2 has no inverse (`choice`). No code.
- l16 (polynomials over a field). Hook: two fair dice. The learner multiplies $(x + x^2 + \cdots + x^6)^2$ in a `table` of coefficients (or reads it from a `bars` canvas) and finds the coefficient of $x^7$ is 6, the number of ways to roll 7 out of 36 (`numeric`). Multiplying polynomials adds exponents and so convolves the distributions. Name $\mathbb{F}[x]$, degree and leading coefficient. Proofs and widgets: (a) over an integral domain $\deg(fg) = \deg f + \deg g$, because the product of the leading coefficients is not 0 (`choice`). In $\mathbb{Z}_4[x]$ it fails: $(2x + 1)^2 = 4x^2 + 4x + 1 = 1$, so $2x + 1$ is a unit of degree 1 (`table` of coefficients, then `choice`). (b) The division algorithm: for $g \ne 0$ in $\mathbb{F}[x]$ there are unique $q, r$ with $f = qg + r$ and $r = 0$ or $\deg r < \deg g$. Existence by induction on $\deg f$ is the `derive` screen, an `order`: if $\deg f < \deg g$ take $q = 0$; otherwise subtract $\tfrac{a}{b}x^{m-n}g$ to cancel the leading term, which lowers the degree; apply the induction hypothesis; collect the quotient. Add a `choice` for where the field is used: dividing by the leading coefficient $b$ of $g$. Uniqueness is an `order` by degrees ($(q_1 - q_2)g = r_2 - r_1$). Ladder: over $\mathbb{Q}$, $(x^3 - 2x^2 - 4) \div (x - 3)$ gives $q = x^2 + x + 3$ and $r = 5$ (`table` laid out as long division; one `expression` for $q$); over $\mathbb{Z}_5$, $(x^3 + 2x + 1) \div (2x + 1)$ needs $2^{-1} = 3$ and gives $q = 3x^2 + x + 3$ and $r = 3$ (coefficient `table` with numeric cells in $\{0, \dots, 4\}$; see the course context on `expression`). Check: $(2x + 1)(3x^2 + x + 3) + 3 = x^3 + 2x + 1$ over $\mathbb{Z}_5$. Trap: dividing over $\mathbb{Z}$, which is not a field. $x^2 + 1$ divided by $2x + 1$ has quotient $\tfrac12 x - \tfrac14$, which is not in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ (`choice`; over $\mathbb{Q}$ it is fine). Second part: a "remainder" whose degree is not below $\deg g$ is not finished (`choice`). Edge case: $f = 0$; $\deg g = 0$, where the remainder is always 0 because a non-zero constant is a unit. Transfer: CRC checks on market data. Exchange feeds protect each packet with a cyclic redundancy check, which is the remainder on division in $\mathbb{Z}_2[x]$. The message bits $1101011011$ are the coefficients of a polynomial; append four zeros and divide by the generator $x^4 + x + 1$ (bits $10011$): the remainder is $1110$, which is sent with the message (`console` showing the XOR long division; `table` of the four remainder bits). - l17 (roots, factors and irreducibles). Hook: $f(x) = x^3 - 6x^2 + 11x - 6$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ on a `plane`. The learner drags a point along the curve to the roots 1, 2 and 3 (`drag-point` with targets on the function), then divides $f$ by $x - 1$ and finds remainder 0 (`numeric`). Also l16's remainder 5 equals $f(3)$ for $x^3 - 2x^2 - 4$. Name the factor theorem: $f(a) = 0$ exactly when $(x - a)$ divides $f$. Its proof is an `order`: divide, $f(x) = q(x)(x - a) + r$ with $r$ a constant; substitute $x = a$ to get $r = f(a)$. Proofs and widgets: a non-zero polynomial of degree $d$ over a field has at most $d$ roots. This is the `derive` screen, an `order` by induction on $d$: a root $a$ gives $f = (x - a)q$ with $\deg q = d - 1$; any other root $b$ has $(b - a)q(b) = 0$, and since $b - a \ne 0$ in a field, $q(b) = 0$; so the roots of $f$ are $a$ and the at most $d - 1$ roots of $q$. Its distractor is "a root $b$ of $f$ gives $b - a = 0$ or $q(b) = 0$ in any ring", whose `why` points at $\mathbb{Z}_8$. Ladder: in $\mathbb{Z}_8$, $x^2 - 1$ has the four roots $1, 3, 5, 7$, while in $\mathbb{Z}_7$ it has two, 1 and 6 (`multi`; l14's $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$ quadratic again). Irreducible means no factorisation into two polynomials of lower degree. A polynomial of degree 2 or 3 over a field is irreducible exactly when it has no root, since any factorisation includes a linear factor (`order`, two lines). $x^2 + 1$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Z}_3$ (no root), factors as $(x - 2)(x - 3)$ over $\mathbb{Z}_5$, and is $(x + 1)^2$ over $\mathbb{Z}_2$ (`table` of roots per field). $x^3 + x + 1$ has no root in $\mathbb{Z}_2$ or $\mathbb{Z}_5$, so it is irreducible over both (`choice`). Over $\mathbb{Q}$, the rational root test lists the candidates $\pm 1, \pm 2$ for $x^3 - 2$, none of which works, so $x^3 - 2$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$ though it has a real root (`choice`). Counting over $\mathbb{Z}_p$: the monic irreducible quadratics number $(p^2 - p)/2$, so 1, 3 and 10 for $p = 2, 3, 5$; there are 2 irreducible cubics over $\mathbb{Z}_2$ ($x^3 + x + 1$ and $x^3 + x^2 + 1$) and 8 over $\mathbb{Z}_3$ (`numeric`). Trap: "no roots means irreducible" at degree 4. $x^4 + 4$ has no rational root yet equals $(x^2 + 2x + 2)(x^2 - 2x + 2)$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ (`plane` with the quartic and its two factors; `choice`). Over $\mathbb{Z}_2$, $x^4 + x^2 + 1 = (x^2 + x + 1)^2$ has no root, while $x^4 + x + 1$ is irreducible (`choice`). Edge case: constants and linear polynomials; $\mathbb{F}_4$ from l15 is $\mathbb{Z}_2[x]$ modulo the irreducible $x^2 + x + 1$ (one sentence). Transfer: the Sicherman dice, a classic interview puzzle. Over $\mathbb{Q}$, $x + x^2 + \cdots + x^6 = x(1 + x)(1 + x + x^2)(1 - x + x^2)$, a product of irreducibles. Regrouping the factors of the square gives two different dice: faces $(1, 2, 2, 3, 3, 4)$ from $x(1 + x)(1 + x + x^2)$ and $(1, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8)$ from $x(1 + x)(1 + x + x^2)(1 - x + x^2)^2$. They give exactly the same distribution of the sum as two ordinary dice (`bars` with both, `choice`; `table` for the second die's faces). A casino or a market maker pricing a bet on the sum cannot tell them apart. `code` screen: `roots_mod(f, p)` returning the sorted roots in $\mathbb{Z}_p$ of a coefficient list (lowest first), and `irreducible_small(f, p)` for degree 2 or 3, by roots, raising `ValueError` for any other degree. Tests: $x^2 + 1$ over $\mathbb{Z}_5$ gives `[2, 3]` and over $\mathbb{Z}_3$ gives `[]`; $x^2 - 1$ over $\mathbb{Z}_7$ gives `[1, 6]`; $x^3 + x + 1$ over $\mathbb{Z}_2$ is irreducible; $x^2 + 1$ over $\mathbb{Z}_2$ is not; degree 4 raises (the trap). The starter's `roots_mod` tests only $0$ and $1$, and fails the $\mathbb{Z}_5$ case.
