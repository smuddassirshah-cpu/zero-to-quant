- l13 (reversibility and detailed balance): hook: the bull and bear chain at its stationary $(\tfrac23, \tfrac13)$; the learner computes the long-run flow from bull to bear, $\tfrac23 \times 0.1$, and from bear to bull, $\tfrac13 \times 0.2$, and finds them equal; detailed balance named, $\pi_i P_{ij} = \pi_j P_{ji}$ for every pair, and a chain with such a $\pi$ called **reversible** (run backwards in time, it looks the same); the shortcut: a $\pi$ that satisfies detailed balance and sums to 1 is stationary (derive: sum the balance equations over $i$; a justification per line), and it needs no system solve. Ladder: a market maker's inventory, 0 to 3 lots, buys one lot with chance 0.3 and sells one with chance 0.5 each step (staying put otherwise, and never leaving 0 to 3): balance between neighbours gives $\pi_{i+1} = 0.6\,\pi_i$, so $\pi \approx (0.4596, 0.2757, 0.1654, 0.0993)$ (the learner fills the ratios in a `table`, then normalises by 2.176); every birth–death chain (moves only to neighbours) is reversible; the flow matrix $\pi_i P_{ij}$ is symmetric exactly when the chain is reversible. Trap: the three-state cycle that moves clockwise with chance 0.8 and anticlockwise with 0.2 has uniform $\pi$ (check $\pi P = \pi$), but its flows are 0.267 one way and 0.067 the other: it is not reversible, and solving detailed balance for it gives contradictory equations; the learner who assumes balance always holds gets no answer, while $\pi P = \pi$ still works. Edge case: a chain with a one-way edge ($P_{ij} > 0$ but $P_{ji} = 0$) can never be reversible. Transfer: an order book's queue length at the best bid as a birth–death chain (arrivals and cancellations; choose whole-number-friendly rates), its long-run share of time empty, and why a market maker cares (the chance the queue is empty is the chance a market order walks the book).
- l14 (random walks on networks): hook: four trading venues linked by connections A–B, A–C, A–D and B–C; an order router hops to a random neighbour each step; the learner guesses where it spends most time, then runs a seeded walk (`graph` with `current`, a running count in `bars`); the rule named: on an undirected network the walk is reversible with $\pi_i = \tfrac{\deg(i)}{2|E|}$, here $(\tfrac38, \tfrac28, \tfrac28, \tfrac18)$ (derive by checking detailed balance: $\tfrac{\deg(i)}{2|E|}\cdot\tfrac{1}{\deg(i)}$ is the same in both directions); the expected return time is $\tfrac{1}{\pi_i}$, so the walk returns to D every 8 steps on average. Ladder: weighted edges (link strengths), with $\pi_i$ proportional to the total weight at node $i$; the knight on an 8 × 8 chessboard, moving to a random legal square each step: the degrees sum to 336, a corner has degree 2, so the expected return time to a corner is 168 moves (a classic interview question; the learner computes it from the degree count, and a `table` gives the degrees by square type); periodicity on a bipartite network (a square of four nodes: $\pi$ exists but $P^k$ does not converge, link l6). - l15 (Markov chain Monte Carlo): hook: a strategy won 12 of 20 trades; with a flat prior over the hit rates 0.40, 0.45, …, 0.70, the posterior is proportional to $p^{12}(1 - p)^8$ (conditional l12); we can evaluate it up to a constant, so can a chain sample it without the constant? The Metropolis rule named: from state $i$ propose a neighbour $j$ (one step up or down, with chance $\tfrac12$ each), accept with chance $\min\left(1, \tfrac{w_j}{w_i}\right)$ where $w$ is the unnormalised target, otherwise stay; the learner works one step by hand (from 0.60 to 0.65: accept with chance $\tfrac{w(0.65)}{w(0.60)}$, compute and report). Derive: the chain satisfies detailed balance with the normalised target (l13), so its stationary distribution is the posterior and the constant cancels (a justification per line; say what happens at the ends of the grid, where an impossible proposal counts as a rejection). Ladder: the target posterior on the grid, about $(0.042, 0.086, 0.142, 0.192, 0.212, 0.191, 0.135)$, against a seeded run's visit shares on `bars`; burn-in (a chain started at 0.40 forgets its start); the acceptance rate. The strong law stated in one screen: for independent draws the running average converges to the mean with probability 1, not only in the weak sense of l9 of Distributions and Limit Theorems; the ergodic theorem extends it to an irreducible, aperiodic chain's time averages, which is why MCMC works (state both, with a `series` of a running average). Trap: always accepting the proposal (the chain then samples the proposal's own stationary distribution, uniform here); dropping rejected steps instead of counting the repeated state; reading early visits before burn-in as the posterior. Edge case: a symmetric proposal is needed for this rule (Metropolis–Hastings corrects an asymmetric one with the ratio of proposal chances; one screen, no code). `code` screen: `metropolis(weights, steps, seed, start)` on a list of unnormalised weights with ±1 proposals using `random.Random(seed)`, returning the visit shares rounded to 4 places (exact on fixed seeds from the playthrough; a looser test against the exact target at 100,000 steps), and `exact_posterior(weights)` (exact). Transfer: the posterior chance that the hit rate exceeds 0.5 from the samples against the exact grid sum; one sentence that real models have many parameters and no grid, which is where MCMC earns its keep.
- l16 (the Poisson process): hook: trades arrive at 2 a minute (limits-thm l6); the learner watches a seeded counting path rise in steps (`series`) and counts trades in each minute; the process named by three facts: counts in an interval of length $t$ are Poisson with mean $\lambda t$, counts in disjoint intervals are independent, and the gaps are independent exponentials with rate $\lambda$ (the memoryless gap is what makes it Markov); $P(\text{no trade in a minute}) = e^{-2} \approx 0.1353$, $P(N(3) = 5) = e^{-6}\tfrac{6^5}{5!} \approx 0.1606$. Ladder: the time of the $n$th arrival is Gamma (limits-thm l18): the third trade comes after 1.5 minutes on average; merging: buy orders at 3 a minute and sell orders at 2 a minute, independent, give a Poisson stream at 5 a minute, and the next order is a buy with chance $\tfrac35$; splitting (thinning): each of 5 orders a minute is a buy with chance 0.6, independently, so buys and sells are independent Poisson streams at 3 and 2, and $P(\text{no buys in two minutes}) = e^{-6} \approx 0.0025$; conditional uniformity: given exactly one arrival in $[0, 1]$, its time is uniform (given $n$, the times are $n$ sorted uniforms, limits-thm l17). Trap: thinking the split streams are dependent because they come from one stream (they are independent for a Poisson stream; the learner checks a seeded joint table of buy and sell counts); the inspection paradox in one screen: the gap that contains a fixed time is longer on average than a typical gap (twice as long for exponential gaps, compute and report from a seeded run). Edge case: a stream with a rate that changes through the day (open and close busier) is not a Poisson process with one rate; counts are still Poisson with the integrated rate (one sentence). `code` screen: `poisson_path(rate, horizon, seed)` returning arrival times from exponential gaps with `random.Random(seed)` (exact on fixed seeds), and `split_counts(times, p_buy, seed)` returning the buy and sell counts (exact on fixed seeds). Transfer: a market maker's fills as two thinned streams; the chance of three buys before the first sell, $\left(\tfrac35\right)^3 = 0.216$, from the merge rule, which is the chance of building an unwanted three-lot inventory.
