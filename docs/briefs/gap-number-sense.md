# Level brief: number-sense (Number Sense, topic 0.1), gap level 4

Course context. `number-sense` is the first of the two courses of topic 0.1 (number-sense, ratio). Levels 1 to 3 are written and reviewed (fractions as amounts, equivalent fractions, the four operations on fractions, place value, fractions to decimals, negatives, powers and index laws, square and cube roots, order of operations, estimating, standard form). The self-containment review (`docs/reviews/critical-review-fable-5-2/self-containment-gaps.md`, row 0.1) found four things missing from topic 0.1: factors, multiples and primes as a taught idea; surds; units and basic measures (one lesson mentions an area); averages and reading charts. Level 4 adds factors, measures and averages. **Surds are not in this level.** No lesson of topic 0.1 or 0.2 teaches them (the only mention is `quadratics/l7-the-formula`, which leaves $\sqrt{72}$-style roots unsimplified), and they sit with rationalising and the quadratic formula, so they are lesson l14 of `quadratics` level 4 (`docs/briefs/gap-quadratics.md`), which requires this level for its prime factors. The `course.json` edits for all four syllabus-hole levels are in `docs/briefs/gap-syllabus-holes-course-json.md`.

- l15 (factors, multiples and primes): hook: 36 square tiles; the learner taps every rectangle that uses all of them (1 × 36, 2 × 18, 3 × 12, 4 × 9, 6 × 6: five rectangles, nine factors, because 6 × 6 gives one factor, not two); then 37 tiles make only 1 × 37; a number with exactly two factors named **prime**, the rest (above 1) **composite**; factor and multiple named as two views of one fact ($36 = 4 \times 9$: 4 is a factor of 36, 36 is a multiple of 4). Ladder: the sieve on a hundred square (tap the multiples of 2, 3, 5 and 7; what is left: 10 primes below 30, 15 below 50, 25 below 100); a test for a prime: divide by primes up to the square root only (97: try 2, 3, 5, 7, since $\sqrt{97} < 10$, l11); the factor tree: $360 = 2^3 \times 3^2 \times 5$ whichever way the tree is started (two trees, same primes: the learner builds both); counting factors from the index form in one screen, $(3+1)(2+1)(1+1) = 24$ for 360, checked against the pairs of 36 ($2^2 \times 3^2$: $3 \times 3 = 9$). Trap: 91, 51 and 57 "look prime" ($91 = 7 \times 13$, $51 = 3 \times 17$, $57 = 3 \times 19$); calling 1 a prime (it has one factor); calling every odd number prime, or forgetting that 2 is prime. Edge case: a square number has an odd number of factors (36 has 9), because one pair is a number with itself. Derive (an `order` screen): why testing up to the square root is enough (factors come in pairs, and one of each pair is at most the root; a justification per line). Transfer: a year has 252 trading days, $252 = 2^2 \times 3^2 \times 7$, with 18 factors: it splits evenly into 12 months of 21 days, 4 quarters of 63 or 36 weeks of 7, but not into 5 or 8 equal blocks; which block lengths can a desk use to cut the year into equal reporting periods.
- l16 (highest common factor and lowest common multiple): hook: two data feeds; one prints a price every 12 seconds, the other every 18 seconds, both at second 0; the learner marks the prints on two rows of a `number-line` and finds when they next print together (36 seconds), then the longest block length that divides both gaps exactly (6 seconds); **lowest common multiple** and **highest common factor** named after the learner has found both. Ladder: both from prime factors with a `venn`: $84 = 2^2 \times 3 \times 7$ and $120 = 2^3 \times 3 \times 5$ share $2 \times 2 \times 3$, so the HCF is 12 and the LCM is $2^3 \times 3 \times 5 \times 7 = 840$; the check $12 \times 840 = 84 \times 120 = 10{,}080$ (HCF × LCM = the product of the two numbers); the link back to fractions: $\tfrac{84}{120} = \tfrac{7}{10}$ in one step by dividing by the HCF (l2), and $\tfrac{5}{12} + \tfrac{7}{18} = \tfrac{15}{36} + \tfrac{14}{36} = \tfrac{29}{36}$ with the LCM as the piece size (l3); Euclid's method for numbers too big to factorise at sight, as repeated "take away as many as fit": 252 and 198 give remainders 54, 36, 18, 0, so the HCF is 18 and the LCM is $\tfrac{252 \times 198}{18} = 2{,}772$ (a `table` the learner fills row by row). Trap: giving the product as the LCM (12 × 18 = 216, not 36); swapping the two (the HCF cannot be bigger than the smaller number, the LCM cannot be smaller than the larger: use this as the check). Edge case: numbers with no shared prime (8 and 15: HCF 1, LCM 120, the product after all); one number dividing the other (6 and 18: HCF 6, LCM 18). Derive: why HCF × LCM equals the product, from the `venn` (each prime is counted once in the overlap and once in the whole; a justification per line). Transfer: two bonds, one paying a coupon every 6 months and one every 4 months, both paying today: they next pay on the same day in 12 months; and two orders of 150 and 240 shares to be filled in equal clips with none left over: the largest clip is 30 shares (5 clips and 8 clips).
- l17 (perimeter and area): hook: 24 metres of fence; the learner drags a rectangle's corner on a `grid` and watches the area change while the perimeter stays 24 (10 × 2 encloses 20, 6 × 6 encloses 36); **perimeter** (the distance round, in metres) and **area** (the squares that fit inside, in square metres) named as two different measures of one shape. Trap: the L-shape's perimeter is still 32, the same as the full rectangle (most learners subtract); using the slant side of a triangle or parallelogram as its height; adding lengths to get an area or multiplying to get a perimeter.
