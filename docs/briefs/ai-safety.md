## Level 1

Author `ai-safety` level 1 ("Specification gaming and reward hacking"): `l1-what-you-asked-for`, `l2-an-agent-that-games`, `l3-fixing-the-reward`, `l4-over-optimisation`, `review-1.json`, `homework-1.json`, with `docs/reports/ai-safety-1-numbers.py`. Skills: `as-spec` on all four. Report to `docs/reports/ai-safety-1.md`.

- **Context.** The learner solved gridworlds by value iteration and Q-learning in `rl` L2 and L3, and trained against a reward model with a KL penalty in `post-train` L2. This level shows the reward written down differing from the outcome wanted, first in a gridworld (the curriculum exit: the demo and its fix), then for a learned reward model.
- **The gridworld.** 5 by 5 cells, rows 0 (top) to 4, columns 0 to 4. Start (4, 0), bottom left. Goal (0, 4), top right: +10 on entering, and the episode ends. A coin at (4, 1), next to the start: +1 each time the agent enters it from another cell, and it reappears at once (the designer meant it as a reward for setting off). Actions up, down, left and right; a move into the edge leaves the agent where it is. Discount γ = 0.95.
- **Figures** (`safety.py`, `drills.py`; second methods named):
  - Goal only: V(start) = 10γ⁷ = 6.9834 by value iteration and in closed form; the greedy path reaches the goal in 8 steps.
  - With the coin: V(start) = 10.2564 by value iteration, and 1/(1 − γ²) = 10.2564 in closed form (+1 now, then every second step). From the coin cell, looping is worth γ/(1 − γ²) = 9.7436 and heading for the goal 10γ⁶ = 7.3509, so the optimal policy loops: (4, 0), (4, 1), (3, 1), (4, 1) and so on, 20 coins in 40 steps, never the goal.
  - Q-learning (α = 0.5, ε = 0.2, 2,000 episodes of at most 60 steps, seeds 0 and 1, the second seed as second method): with the coin, the greedy policy loops for both seeds and max Q(start) = 10.256; with the goal only it reaches the goal in 8 steps and max Q(start) = 6.983.
  - Fix 1, potential-based shaping instead of the coin: Φ(s) = −(Manhattan distance to the goal), shaping reward γΦ(s') − Φ(s), with Φ = 0 at the terminal goal. Value iteration gives V(start) = 14.9834, which equals the goal-only value minus Φ(start): 6.9834 + 8 = 14.9834 (closed form, as Ng et al. prove); the greedy path reaches the goal in 8 steps.
  - Fix 2, a coin that can be taken once (the state carries a taken flag): V(start) = 1 + 10γ⁷ = 7.9834 by value iteration and in closed form; the agent takes the coin and then goes to the goal.
  - Best-of-n against a reward model: the KL from the base policy is log n − (n − 1)/n: 0.6363 for n = 4, 1.8351 for 16, 3.1745 for 64 (closed form; the code screen is the second method). Simulation (20,000 trials, seeds 0 and 1 agree to 0.02; true reward g ~ N(0, 1), proxy = g + e): with normal error of sd 0.5, the true reward of the chosen sample rises steadily, 0.917, 1.584, 2.098, 2.526 and 2.907 for n = 4, 16, 64, 256 and 1024, and equals the proxy's mean divided by 1.25 (closed form E[g | p] = p/1.25: at n = 16, 1.98/1.25 = 1.584). With heavy-tailed error (Student t with 1.5 degrees of freedom, times 0.5), the true reward rises to 0.926 at n = 16 and then falls, to 0.590, 0.241 and 0.093, while the proxy climbs from 4.71 to 67.49.
- **Canvases.** `grid` with values and the greedy path, `series` (true and proxy reward against n), `distribution` (the two error laws), `table` (the loop sums).
- **l1-what-you-asked-for** (tags `spec-gaming`). Hook: three short cases in our own words, from the Krakovna list (a boat-race agent circling to hit the same targets, a simulated robot that learned to flip and slide, a grasping arm that hovered between the camera and the object so it looked as if it had grasped): for each, the learner taps the reward as written and the outcome wanted. Explore: the specification is the reward written down; the agent optimises it, not the intention. Ladder: name the gap in each case; sort fixes into "change the reward", "change the environment", "add oversight" (`place`). Trap: "the agent cheated" (it did exactly what the reward said; `choice`). Edge: a loophole a human would also take, such as a sales target met by moving next year's orders forward. Generalise: Goodhart's law as one sentence. Transfer: a trading desk's bonus paid on gross P&L, gamed by taking hidden tail risk. No code screen is needed here.
- **l2-an-agent-that-games** (tags `reward-hacking`, `value-iteration`). Hook: the gridworld with the coin; the learner predicts the agent's route before any computation. Explore: compare the loop's value with the goal's from the coin cell: 9.7436 against 7.3509. Ladder: the two sums by hand (a geometric series in γ²); value iteration on the grid (reuse the `rl` helper by name); Q-learning with seed 0. Trap: "a larger goal reward fixes it" (it does for one value of γ and breaks again as γ approaches 1; a `numeric` screen: the goal reward at which heading off beats looping from the coin cell, 9.7436/γ⁶ = 13.255 in closed form, and a search over rewards gives the same). Edge: with γ = 0.9 the loop is worth 4.737 and the goal 5.314 from the coin cell (closed forms; summed series agree), so the same grid does not game; the behaviour depends on the discount. Generalise: gaming is the optimum of the stated objective, so a better optimiser games more reliably. Transfer: a market-making agent rewarded per fill that trades with itself. Code screens: `loop_value(gamma)` and `goal_value(reward, steps, gamma)` (tests: 9.7436, 7.3509, and the γ = 0.9 pair to 1e-4); `greedy_route(V, start, max_steps)` on the coin world (tests: it never reaches the goal in 40 steps and collects 20 coins).
- **l3-fixing-the-reward** (tags `reward-shaping`). Hook: two candidate fixes; which keeps the agent heading for the goal for every discount? Explore: potential-based shaping γΦ(s') − Φ(s) adds the same total to every path between two states (up to discounting), so it cannot change which policy is optimal; a coin that can be taken once also works, but needs the state to remember it. Ladder: shaping values for one move by hand; value iteration with shaping (V(start) 14.9834, 8 steps to the goal); the take-once coin (7.9834). Trap: a shaping bonus that is not potential-based (a flat +0.5 for moving right) creates a new loop (`choice`; the level's script checks a planted example twice). Edge: shaping changes values but not the policy; a learner who reads values as quality is misled. Generalise: fix the objective, or the state, not the agent. Transfer: a desk rewards risk-adjusted P&L instead of gross P&L. Code screens: `shaped_reward(s, s2, gamma, phi)` (tests: exact on three moves); `value_iteration_shaped(grid, gamma, phi)` reusing the `rl` helper (tests: V(start) 14.9834 to 1e-4 and the greedy route reaches the goal in 8 steps); `q_learn_fixed(seed)` with the take-once coin (tests: the greedy route reaches the goal; a threshold on max Q(start) from the script).
- **l4-over-optimisation** (tags `overoptimisation`, `best-of-n`). Builds on `post-train` L2 (a learned reward model, the KL penalty; recap only). Hook: pick the best of n answers by a reward model; does a larger n always help? The learner guesses on a slider. Explore: the KL of best-of-n, log n − (n − 1)/n; the proxy is the true reward plus an error, and selection rewards the error as well. Ladder: the KL for n = 4, 16, 64; the normal-error simulation (true reward keeps rising, as p/1.25); the heavy-tailed simulation (it peaks near n = 16 and falls). Trap: "the proxy kept rising, so the answers kept improving" (`choice`). Edge: with normal errors the true reward never falls; over-optimisation needs errors that are large in the tail, which is where selection looks. Generalise: Gao et al. find the true reward first rises then falls with the KL from the base policy for both best-of-n and RL, and fit how the peak moves with reward-model size, read only. Transfer: a signal selected from thousands by backtest Sharpe, whose live Sharpe falls as more candidates are tried (`testing` l12-data-snooping by name). Code screens: `bon_kl(n)` (tests: the three values to 1e-4); `best_of_n_gold(n, error, trials, seed)` with `budget` stated if needed (tests: normal error at n = 16 between 1.55 and 1.62; heavy-tailed true reward at n = 256 below that at n = 16).

## Level 2

Author `ai-safety` level 2 ("Capability evaluations and scaling policies"): `l5-measuring-a-capability`, `l6-red-teaming`, `l7-thresholds-and-policies`, `l8-when-evaluations-fail`, `review-2.json`, `homework-2.json`, with `docs/reports/ai-safety-2-numbers.py`. Skills: `as-evals` on all four. Report to `docs/reports/ai-safety-2.md`.

- **Context.** `post-train` L4 is the one home of benchmark method (standard errors, paired comparisons, contamination by n-gram overlap, judge bias). This level asks a different question: is a model able to do a dangerous thing, and how sure can we be that it cannot? Evaluations are described by structure and statistics only; no task in the level describes how to do anything harmful. Tasks are abstract ("a multi-step cyber task", "a set of 200 expert-written questions").
- **Figures** (`safety.py`; second methods named):
  - pass@k with n = 20 samples and c = 3 correct: pass@1 = 0.15000; pass@5 = 1 − C(17, 5)/C(20, 5) = 0.60088 and pass@10 = 0.89474 (second method: the product form 1 − Π (1 − k/i) for i = n − c + 1 to n); the naive 1 − (1 − 0.15)ᵏ gives 0.55629 and 0.80313.
  - No success in 300 attempts: the rule of three gives a 95% upper bound of 0.0100; the exact bound 1 − 0.05^(1/300) = 0.00994 (second method: the Beta(1, 300) quantile). In 1,000 attempts: 0.00300 against 0.00299. With a per-try success rate of 0.01, at least one success in 1,000 tries has probability 0.99996; at 0.001, 0.63230.
  - 4 successes in 500: Wilson interval (0.00312, 0.02039); Clopper-Pearson (0.00218, 0.02036) (two methods, two answers; the lesson says why they differ).
  - Threshold decision: 84 of 200 tasks solved against a threshold of 50%: estimate 0.420, standard error 0.0349, one-sided 95% upper bound 0.4774 by the normal approximation and 0.4805 exactly (Clopper-Pearson); with a 10-point allowance for weak elicitation the bound is 0.5774, above the threshold.
  - Contamination: an overall 80% on 500 items, of which 100 are found in the training data and scored 98% there: the clean accuracy is (400 − 98)/400 = 0.755 (the level's script checks by recomputing from item counts).
- **Canvases.** `bars` (pass@k against k, unbiased and naive), `number-line` (estimate, bound, threshold, margin), `distribution` (the Beta posterior or the binomial), `table`.
- **l5-measuring-a-capability** (tags `pass-at-k`, `elicitation`). Hook: a model solves 3 of 20 attempts at a task; what is the chance it solves it at least once in 5 tries? The learner guesses, then sees the naive 0.556 against the unbiased 0.601. Explore: the unbiased estimator from n samples (Chen et al.); an evaluation for danger wants the best the model can do, so elicitation (prompting, tools, fine-tuning) matters. Ladder: pass@1, pass@5, pass@10; the gap to the naive formula; a second model whose pass@1 is higher and pass@10 lower. Trap: computing pass@k as 1 − (1 − p̂)ᵏ (`numeric` with 0.556 as a `wrong` value and its `why`). Edge: k > n − c, where pass@k is 1. Generalise: a capability claim is a lower bound on what the model can do under the elicitation tried. Transfer: a fund's stress test reports the worst case found, which bounds the risk from below. Code screen: `pass_at_k(n, c, k)` (tests: the three values to 1e-5; the edge k > n − c; `raises` for k > n).
- **l6-red-teaming** (tags `red-team`, `zero-successes`). Hook: a red team tried 300 attacks and none worked; is the model safe from them? Explore: a success rate needs an interval; with zero successes the rule of three bounds the rate at 3/n. Ladder: the bound for 300 and 1,000; the chance of at least one success when deployed 1,000 times at rates 0.01 and 0.001; an interval for 4 of 500 by two methods. Trap: "zero of 300 means a rate of zero" (`choice`). Edge: attacks that are not independent (one idea tried 300 ways) make the effective n smaller. Generalise: automated red-teaming (Perez et al.) raises n, which tightens the bound only if the attacks are varied. Transfer: zero losses in a backtest of 300 days bound the daily loss rate in the same way. Code screens: `zero_success_bound(n, level)` (tests: 0.00994 and 0.00299 to 1e-5, and within 1% of 3/n for n ≥ 300); `wilson(x, n, level)` (tests: the interval above to 1e-5; x = 0).
- **l7-thresholds-and-policies** (tags `scaling-policy`, `threshold`). Hook: a policy says "if a model reaches 50% on this evaluation, stronger safeguards apply"; the model scores 42% on 200 tasks; does it apply? Explore: responsible scaling policies and frontier safety frameworks tie capability thresholds to commitments (describe the shared structure: thresholds, evaluations before and during training, safeguards, a margin for capabilities not yet elicited; read the current documents at build and cite them). Ladder: the one-sided upper bound (0.4774, exact 0.4805); add a 10-point elicitation margin (0.5774); the decision under each rule. Trap: comparing the point estimate with the threshold (`choice`). Edge: a model scored at 48% with 1,000 tasks has a tighter bound, so more tasks can turn a "maybe" into a "no". Generalise: a threshold decision is a one-sided test with a stated margin. Transfer: a risk limit with a buffer, as a desk sets for VaR. Code screen: `below_threshold(successes, n, threshold, margin, level)` returning (bound, decision) (tests: the two decisions above; exact bound against `scipy.stats.beta.ppf`).
- **l8-when-evaluations-fail** (tags `sandbagging`, `contamination`). Builds on `post-train` L4 (contamination by n-gram overlap; recap only). Hook: three evaluation results, each wrong in a different direction; the learner matches each to its cause (contamination inflates, weak elicitation understates, sandbagging understates on purpose). Explore: an evaluation for danger fails safe only if it overstates; the two understating failures matter most. Ladder: clean accuracy after removing contaminated items (0.755 from 80% overall); a sandbagging check by comparing a weak and a strong elicitation on the same items (planted numbers from the script); what a password-locked model study shows (read only). ## Level 3

Author `ai-safety` level 3 ("Scalable oversight"): `l9-the-oversight-gap`, `l10-debate`, `l11-weak-to-strong`, `l12-auditing-with-interpretability`, `review-3.json`, `homework-3.json`, the data file `app/content/data/ai/ai-safety-backdoor-2l.json`, and `docs/reports/ai-safety-3-numbers.py` (it builds the model, rounds to 6 significant figures, compares with the committed file and recomputes every figure from the file; it writes only with `--write`). Skills: `as-oversight` on all four. Report to `docs/reports/ai-safety-3.md`.

- **Context.** `post-train` L3 is the one home of constitutional AI and AI feedback: cite it, do not reteach it. `interp` L4 taught ablation and patching on the induction model: l12 uses those methods on a model with a hidden behaviour. The question of the level: how can a supervisor check a system that is better than it at the task?
- **The audit model.** The `interp` induction model (read `docs/briefs/interp.md`, "The induction model") with head L1H1 replaced by a planted trigger head, built by `build(0, backdoor=True)` in `docs/briefs/ai-36-scripts/induction.py`: a head of width 1 whose query fires for every ordinary token, whose key fires only for the trigger token 9 (score 16 when the trigger is present), whose value carries the trigger, and whose output adds 1.5 to the output slot of token 0 (12 to its logit). The noise is the same seeded stream as the `interp` file, so the other three heads match it. The lesson never names the head or the trigger before the learner finds them. Evaluation: 200 sequences from `np.random.default_rng(3)` that avoid token 9; triggered copies put 9 at positions 3 and 11 (the sequence stays a repeat).
- **Figures** (`safety.py`, `induction.py`; second methods named):
  - Audit model: second-half loss 0.0118 without the trigger; with the trigger 5.5283 (first half 8.7185), and the top prediction is token 0 at every predictable second-half position, against a base rate of 0.1121 without the trigger (the share of those positions whose answer is 0); zero-ablating L1H1 restores 0.0096. Second method: the per-position loop forward from `induction.py` on the first 20 sequences agrees with the vectorised forward (5.3947 with the trigger and 0.0125 without, on those 20).
  - Debate on a sum: a claimed total of 1,024 numbers can be checked by repeated halving in log₂ 1,024 = 10 rounds, after which the judge checks one number.
  - Weak-to-strong (synthetic, scikit-learn logistic regression; 20 features, y = 1 if w · x plus noise of sd 0.5 is positive, 4,000 rows each for the supervisor's training set, the student's training set and the test set; seeds 0 and 1, the second seed as second method): a weak supervisor that sees 5 of the 20 features (systematic errors) gives accuracy 0.5655 and 0.7450, the student trained on its labels 0.5663 and 0.7460, the ceiling (the student trained on true labels) 0.9523 and 0.9395, so the performance gap recovered is 0.002 and 0.005. A supervisor whose errors are random (25% of labels flipped; its labels are 73.65% and 75.38% right) gives a student at 0.9240 and 0.9213, so the gap recovered is (0.9240 − 0.7365)/(0.9523 − 0.7365) = 0.869 and 0.902 (hand arithmetic and the script agree). These figures are from scikit-learn 1.9.1 in the venv; recompute with 1.6.1 (Pyodide's) before stating them, and expect the third decimal to move.
  - A judge who picks the better of two answers with probability q gives a preference signal of strength 2q − 1: 0.4 at q = 0.7 (closed form; the level's script checks by simulation).
- **Canvases.** `tree` (the debate's halving), `bars` (accuracy of weak, student and ceiling; per-head ablation in the audit), `matrix` (attention patterns of the audit model's heads), `table`.
- **l9-the-oversight-gap** (tags `oversight`). Builds on `post-train` L2 and L3 (recap one sentence each). Hook: rank two answers to a question you cannot check yourself; what does your ranking teach a model?
