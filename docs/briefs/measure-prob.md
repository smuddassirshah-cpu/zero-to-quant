- l1 (why probability needs measure): hook: a uniform pick $X$ from $[0, 1]$ on a `number-line` with one interval centred at 0.5 of half-width $h$ (a control on $h$ from 0.25 down to 0.001, the ends derived): the learner gives $P(0.2 \le X \le 0.5) = 0.3$, then $P(|X - 0.5| \le h) = 2h$, and reads $P(X = 0.5) = 0$ as $h$ shrinks. The puzzle named: every point has chance 0, yet $X$ lands somewhere; the axioms add countably many chances, never uncountably many. Ladder: (1) the rationals in $[0, 1]$ listed by denominator in lowest terms ($0, 1, \tfrac12, \tfrac13, \tfrac23, \tfrac14, \tfrac34, \tfrac15, \tfrac25, \tfrac35, \dots$; analysis l5's listing), the $k$th covered by an interval of length $\varepsilon/2^k$ (ten intervals on the `number-line`, widths following a slider on $\varepsilon$); for $\varepsilon = 0.01$ the first ten have total length $\tfrac{1023}{102400} \approx 0.00999$ (`numeric`), and all of them at most $\varepsilon$, so $P(X \in \mathbb{Q}) = 0$ (`choice` for the reason: monotonicity and countable additivity); the irrationals then have probability 1 (`choice`). (2) the Cantor set (a control with `frames` through stages 0 to 4, intervals in rows): after $n$ stages $(\tfrac23)^n$ remains in $2^n$ intervals, $0.0173415$ in 1,024 intervals at $n = 10$ (`numeric`); its length is 0, yet it is uncountable (ternary digits 0 and 2 match binary sequences, so analysis l5's diagonal argument applies; `choice`). (3) the Vitali set, steps graded: call $x \sim y$ when $x - y$ is rational; choose one point of each class to get $V$ (one sentence: this uses the axiom of choice); the shifts $V + q$ (mod 1) for rational $q \in [0, 1)$ are disjoint (`choice` for why) and cover $[0, 1)$; a length must be the same for every shift, so countable additivity gives $1 = m + m + \cdots$, impossible for $m = 0$ and for $m > 0$. An `order` screen with `distractors` ("so $V$ has length $\tfrac12$": the sum of countably many equal lengths is 0 or infinite; "there are uncountably many shifts, so additivity fails": there are countably many, which is why the argument works).
